167The applicants have averred that there is a serious issue to be tried because
168the respondents have allegedly committed a sub judice contempt of court by
169publishing a video of the press conference. It is their contention that the press
170conference broadcasted in the video contains comments which relate to matters
171which are pending before the Supreme Court and are calculated to interfere with the
172course of justice and tarnish the image of the judiciary by equating the legitimate
173recourse to legal proceedings to acts of “persekisyon” and that their reputation will be
174affected. They have also averred that if the injunction is not granted, the videos will
175be viewed by many persons and will cause irremediable prejudice
176Before considering whether there is a serious issue to be tried, I find it
177apposite to refer to the following extract regarding sub judice contempt from Media
178Law Handbook, Joseph M. Hernandez, 5th Edition, 2007, at pages 142 and 143-
179“ 1. Sub judice contempts
180“Sub judice” means “under a judge”. That is, the matter discussed by
181the media is still being considered by a court of law; and it is not yet
182decided. One definition of the aim of the sub judice contempt rule is to
183stop the publication of material that might prejudice court proceedings
184while they are pending. That is, they are still under consideration by
185the courts. Proceedings are not sub judice unless they are pending.
186This includes cases that are heard by judge alone, or by judge and
187jury. However, it is where juries are involved that greater caution is
188necessary, as the assumption is that juries are more likely to be
189influenced by the media. Most contempt by publication cases involve
190publications that may prejudice jury trials. The following is a useful
191summary of the rule’s rationale:
192[W]hat the sub judice rule seeks to do is to filter out the most
193damaging of prejudicial effects. It seeks to ensure that views formed
194prior to the trial, or from extrinsic sources during the trial, are not held
195so strongly that they cannot be displaced by the evidence that is
196presented and tested in the courtroom, judicial directions and
197instructions, and arguments and submissions by counsel. It seeks to
198suppress only that material which has a real and definite tendency, as
199a matter of practical reality, to prejudice legal proceedings.”
200It can be gathered from the above that the sub judice rule also applies to
201cases heard by a judge in the absence of a jury and that the rationale behind the sub
202judice rule is to prevent the formation of views by the court which “cannot be
203displaced by the evidence that is presented and tested in the courtroom, judicial
204directions and instructions, and arguments and submissions by counsel”.

207In the light of what has been stated above, I find that there is a serious issue
208to be tried between the parties.
209Insofar as the question as to whether damages will be an adequate remedy is
210concerned, I note that although the applicants have stated in their affidavits that the
211reputation of applicant No. 1 will be adversely affected, from the tenor of the
212applicants affidavits their main qualm seems to be that the videos are calculated to
213interfere with the course of justice and tarnish the image of the judiciary by equating
214the legitimate recourse to legal proceedings to acts of “persekisyon”. I also note that
215the applicants have not sought any damages in the main cases lodged against the
216respondents: In the “main case” entered against respondents No. 1 and 2, applicant
217No. 1 is asking the Court to make a finding that the comments and remarks made by
218respondents No. 1 and 2 during the press conference are calculated to interfere with
219the course of justice in connection with proceedings which they knew were pending,
220that by making those comments they have brought the court’s process into disrepute
221and are attempting to intimidate the court. The respondents are also being asked to
222show cause why they should not be dealt according to law for contempt. In the case
223against respondent No. 4, applicant No. 1 is asking the Court to make a finding that
224by publishing videos of the press conference on the website of Le Mauricien
225Newspaper, together with an Article, and on its Youtube channel, respondent No. 4
226has committed a sub judice contempt of court. It is also asking respondent No. 4 to
227show cause why it should not be dealt with according to law for contempt of court. In
228the circumstances, damages will clearly not be an adequate remedy.
229As regards the issue of balance of convenience, I note that the Director of
230Public Prosecutions has chosen not to initiate any action for contempt against the
231respondents. Further, if the application is not granted but the respondents are found
232to have indeed committed a contempt in the main cases lodged against them, the
233Court will surely mete out the appropriate penalty to the respondents by taking into
234consideration the length of time during which the contempt has been ongoing. On the
235other hand, if the application is granted but the main case is decided against the
236applicants, the respondents will have been deprived of their right to freedom of
237expression, which is a sacrosanct right protected under our Constitution.
238It is trite law that constitutional rights cannot be interfered with lightly; there
239needs to be a very clear and strong case indeed before the Judge in Chambers
240warranting the curtailing of the fundamental rights of a person.
241
2428
243In the light of the above, I therefore find that the balance of convenience is
244not in favour of the granting of the application.
245For all the reasons given above, I refuse to grant the injunction and set aside
246the application. With costs.

CASE
Le Petit Morne Ltée & Anor v Dholah V & Ors
2020 SCJ 229
Judgment delivered 28 September 2020 (In Chambers; Cause No. 1360/19)

COURT & BENCH
The Supreme Court of Mauritius, sitting in Chambers (Judge in Chambers), at first instance on an original application. K.D. Gunesh-Balaghee, J., sitting alone; judgment delivered by Gunesh-Balaghee J. No separate or dissenting judgment.

FACTS
Applicant No. 1 claimed ownership of several portions of land at Le Morne, including a portion of 43 arpents; Applicant No. 2 was its director. Respondents No. 1 and 2 were leaders of the political party Rezistans ek Alternativ ("REA"); Respondent No. 3 provided broadcasting services; Respondent No. 4 owned and published the daily newspaper "Le Mauricien". Applicant No. 1 had sought and obtained a number of injunctions, and had entered contempt proceedings, against inhabitants of Le Morne — including Louis Fabrice ("Yony") Auguste and Charlène Labonne — concerning alleged trespass on and interference with its proprietary rights over the land. On 10 July 2019 REA and associated groups held a press conference at which speakers characterised the pending legal proceedings as "persekisyon"; a video was published on REA's Facebook page and on Le Mauricien's website, YouTube channel and in an accompanying article.

The applicants applied to the Judge in Chambers for an interim injunction restraining the respondents from broadcasting the video and from commenting on or publishing material connected to the pending cases, contending that publication amounted to a sub judice contempt of court calculated to interfere with the course of justice. The interim order was declined and a summons to show cause was issued. After an exchange of affidavits, the applicants did not press the case against Respondent No. 3 and abandoned all prayers save that restraining broadcast of the video, so that the sole issue was whether to grant an interlocutory injunction restraining Respondents No. 1, 2 and 4 from broadcasting a full or partial video of the press conference. The respondents relied on the right to freedom of expression under section 12 of the Constitution.

HELD
1. Although there was a serious issue to be tried and damages would not have been an adequate remedy, the interlocutory injunction was refused because the balance of convenience did not favour granting it (pp. 7–8).
2. The balance of convenience lay against the grant: the Director of Public Prosecutions had chosen not to initiate any contempt action against the respondents; if the respondents were found in the pending main cases to have committed contempt, the court could impose an appropriate penalty having regard to the length of time the contempt had been ongoing; whereas granting the injunction would deprive the respondents of the constitutionally protected freedom of expression, which cannot be curtailed without a very clear and strong case (pp. 7–8).

Injunction refused; application set aside with costs.

POINTS OF LAW
1. On an application for an interlocutory injunction the applicant must satisfy the court that there is a serious issue to be tried, that damages would not be an adequate remedy, and that the balance of convenience favours the grant; the application fails if any limb is not met.
2. Where the grant of an injunction would restrain the exercise of the constitutional right to freedom of expression, the balance of convenience requires a very clear and strong case before that right is curtailed; constitutional rights are not to be interfered with lightly.
3. (obiter) The sub judice rule of contempt applies not only to jury trials but also to proceedings heard by a judge sitting alone, the assumption being that a jury is more readily influenced; its rationale is to suppress only material with a real and definite tendency to prejudice pending proceedings (stated in support of the serious-issue limb, which was not decisive; drawn from Media Law Handbook, J.M. Hernandez, 5th ed., 2007, pp. 142–143) (pp. 6–7).
