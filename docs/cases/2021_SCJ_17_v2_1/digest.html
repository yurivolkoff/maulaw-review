19I have declined in this matter to issue an interim injunction applied for on the 16 October
202019 against applicant’s employer (the respondent), its Executive Director (the first co-
21respondent) and (the three co-respondents) who are also members of the disciplinary
22committee (Me Ravin Chetty, Mr Candhayalallsing Seebaluck and Mr Sailesh Ramyead) and
23instead issued a summons calling upon the respondent and co-respondents to show cause why
24an order in the nature of an interim injunction should not be granted.
25The injunctive order prayed for is to restrain further disciplinary proceedings against the
26applicant, as the Confidential Personal Assistant of the Competition Commission (respondent).
27
282
29It transpires from the affidavits and documents filed by the applicant that she was
30employed by the respondent as Confidential Personal Assistant since the 4 January 2010. She
31was suspended from work on the 23 August 2019 under section 38(2) (a) (iii) of the
32Employment Rights Act under the following charge:
33“On Thursday 15 August 2019, you failed to record receipt of an envelope in the
34incoming register of the Competition Commission on the date of its receipt being
35Thursday 15 August 2019 and to inform your superiors of the arrival of the said
36envelope, the more so that the said envelope contained a proposal for a tender
37exercise, for which the closing date was of the same day at 14hrs. You only
38remitted the envelope in the afternoon of Friday 16 August 2019 to Mr V Keenoo,
39Assistant to the Executive Director.”
40The respondent convened the applicant to attend a disciplinary committee on the 17
41October 2019 at 2.30 pm at the seat of the Competition Commission. However, on the same
42day Me Ghose – Radhakeesoon appearing for the respondent and co-respondent No. 1
43instructing Me A. Domingue, SC gave an undertaking that there will be no disciplinary
44committee pending the determination of the present application. The undertaking was renewed
45by Me A. Domingue, SC on the 16 July 2020. The co-respondent Nos. 2, 3 and 4 will abide by
46the decision of the Court. Amongst the various complaints raised by the applicant was interalia
47the composition of the disciplinary committee.
48According to the Human Resources Manual of the Competition Commission of
49Mauritius;
50Section 8 Disciplinary procedures:
51‘Where it appears, an employee has committed a misconduct;
52(iii) Notify the employee in writing of the alleged offence and of a hearing
53(through a disciplinary committee where necessary, that would include one
54senior CCM staff member, one Commissioner and one outsider).’
55The applicant has averred in her affidavit that the disciplinary committee is composed of
56co-respondent No. 2 as President and co-respondent Nos. 3 and 4 as members. Co-
57respondent No. 3 is a Commissioner of the respondent and will be called upon to decide, upon
58receipt of the findings or recommendations of the said disciplinary committee whether to
59terminate her employment.
60She avers that co-respondent No. 4 who is the Head of Investigations of the respondent
61and works upon instructions of and under the supervision of the co-respondent No. 1, is the one
62who had issued the letters for her suspension and convocation before the disciplinary
63committee.
64
653
66It is the contention of the applicant at paragraph 20 of her affidavit dated 16 October
672019 ‘that the inclusion of co-respondent Nos. 3 and 4 as members of the said disciplinary
68committee is legally flawed as there is a ‘real perception of bias’ and as such (she) fears that the
69principle of natural justice will not prevail and she will not be guaranteed a fair hearing’.
70Co-respondent No.3, having decided the disciplinary charge and after having taken
71cognizance of the applicant’s defence, cannot sit on the disciplinary committee to hear the same
72charge against the applicant.
73Learned Counsel for the applicant relied on the case of Karuthasami M v Equilibre Ltd
74[2013 SCJ 338] where it was held that ‘the composition of the disciplinary Committee was not
75challenged by the appellant, who was legally assisted at the hearing. In the circumstances, the
76appellant’s contention that he was denied a fair hearing is untenable’ and on the case of Robert
77Tranquille v P.R Limited [1996 SCJ 366] where it was held that ‘the notion of fair hearing also
78comprises the idea that a person who may appear to be biased should not sit on the disciplinary
79committee’.
80The applicant confirmed in Court that no main case for leave to apply for Judicial Review
81has been entered as the injunctive relief if granted by the Court will give the applicant a complete
82remedy making it unnecessary to enter a main case. (vide- Beerjeeraz v United Basalt
83Products [1990 MR 159] and Automatic Systems Ltd v Komarsingh C.K [2007 SCJ 319].
84The respondent on its part, raised a number of preliminary objections in law, which
85can be summed up as follows:
86(a) Damages would be an adequate remedy. The applicant has noticeably
87failed to provide any undertaking as to damages which is fatal to the present
88application.
89(b) There is no main action (which could only conceivably have been by a
90prompt application in public law for leave to apply for a Judicial Review).
91(c) The applicant holds no legal right capable of being enforced by an injunction.
92(d) There is no triable issue.
93It is the contention of the respondent in paragraph (a) of the above that:
94“ …. damages and not an injunction is the proper remedy in virtually every case
95of breach of contract, especially one relating to master and servant. The basis of
96this rule is the need for mutual trust and confidence” (vide- Niole v MRA [2015
97SCJ 428]).
98The respondent has also raised in paragraph (b) of its preliminary objections that the
99present application does not disclose any public law element capable of supporting a main
100
1014
102action for leave to apply for Judicial Review as the basic requirement of promptitude has not
103been complied. Learned Counsel for the respondent quoted the case of Mauritius Shipping
104Corporation Ltd (Appellant) v Employment Relations Tribunal and others (Respondents)
105(Mauritius) 2019 UKPC 42 where the requirement of promptness was explained as follows by
106the Court:
107“The requirement of promptness
1087. An application for judicial review must be made promptly, and in any event
109within three months from the date when the grounds for the application first
110arose. The Supreme Court cited, in this regard, the cases of Karamuth v
111Universal Hotels Ltd [1992] SCJ 420, Bagha v The Public Service Commission
112[1996] SCJ 146 and Securiclean (Mauritius) Ltd v The Ministry of Local
113Government & Outer Islands [2015] SCJ 327.
1148. It is important to bear in mind that an applicant cannot rely upon having three
115months in which to launch a judicial review application. The primary requirement
116is that the application be made promptly. The requirement that it be made in any
117event within three months is a “longstop”. It is well established, and the appellant
118accepts, that, depending on the circumstances of the case, an application made
119within three months may not qualify as having been made promptly, in which
120case, leave to apply for judicial review may be refused.’’
121With regard to the objection raised by the respondent in paragraphs (c) and (d) as to
122whether the applicant holds no legal right capable of being enforced by an injunction, learned
123Counsel relied on the dictum in the case of Bhoolah v ICTA [2016 SCJ 414] where the Court
124held as follows:
125“The general rule is that an injunction will not be granted to restrain an employer
126from terminating a contract of employment. Instead, the employee can sue the
127employee for damages for breach of contract of employment. The basic reason
128for refusing an injunction is that the relationship between employer and employee
129is one of confidence, and the court ought not to seek to prolong the relationship
130by an injunction or a decree of specific performance when that confidence no
131longer exists. If the employee does not want to employ the employee, the
132employee does not want to work for the employer, you cannot by order of the
133Court satisfactorily make them do what one of them does not want to do.
134(emphasis added).”
135In reply to the issue relative to the composition of the disciplinary committee, the
136respondent submitted that the precepts of the Human Resources Management Manual of the
137Competition Commission have been scrupulously adhered by the respondent as per paragraph
1388(iii) of the Manual (Disciplinary Procedures).
139In its counter affidavit dated the 14 November 2019, the respondent averred at
140paragraphs 16 and 17, “that upon receipt of the findings of the disciplinary committee, the co-
141respondent No. 3 will be functus officio and will not be entitled to participate in any further
142deliberations of the Commission in relation with the disciplinary proceedings. Whereas co-
143
1445
145respondent No. 4 has been validly appointed in accordance to the Manual and the
146independence of the members are not impaired.”
147To support this averment, the respondent referred to the case of Govinden v Riche -
148en- Eau Co-operative Society [1997 SCJ 265] holding that; “there was no impropriety in the
149fact that the Chairman of the respondent who acted as the representative of the Committee
150which drafted the charges, also sitting on the committee to hear the charges although he had
151sent all the letters to the appellant, in view of the informal character of the disciplinary
152committee.’’
153In the case of Cooraban v MIE [1995 SCJ 271] quoted by the respondent, the
154appellant’s first complaint is that he did not get a fair hearing since Mr. Deeljore was a member
155of the disciplinary committee and he was also one of the three persons who had requested
156Surnam to sign his letter in December 1993. The Appellate Court had this to say:
157“We do not find that the mere fact of Mr Deeljore requesting Surnam to put his
158complaint against the applicant in writing disqualified him, as the Registrar of the
159respondent, to sit on the disciplinary committee, along with other officers, to hear
160the applicant’s case. As it has been said before a disciplinary committee is not a
161Court of law and it is often difficult to find committee members who are totally
162unaware of complaints which have to be heard.”
163I propose to deal firstly with the issue relating to the composition of the disciplinary
164committee in the light of the argument of learned counsel for the applicant and the respondent
165and the authorities they have alluded to.
166The applicant has confirmed that she is not seeking an injunction against the holding of
167the disciplinary proceedings but is challenging the composition of its members.
168It is my considered view that the applicant has seized the wrong forum, as objection as
169to the composition of the disciplinary committee should have been taken before the committee
170itself and cannot be invoked at this stage.
171In the case of Ramsewak R v MBC [1994 SCJ 297], there was an application for an
172interim order in the nature of an interlocutory injunction restraining and prohibiting the
173respondent from instituting disciplinary proceedings against the applicant. According to the
174applicant, he will not be given a fair hearing since the members of the disciplinary committee
175were the very ones who had compelled him to take 6 month’s leave. It was submitted on behalf
176of the respondent that, the application was refused by the Court for the following reasons: -
177“(1) The respondent is empowered to sanction the misconduct of its employee,
178subject of course to the supervision and control of the Court. See (1) The United
179Bus Service Ltd v. Gokhool [1978 MR 1] and (2) Ismael v. The United Bus
180
1816
182Service Ltd [1986 SCJ 303]. But, before any sanction is taken the respondent is
183required to hold a disciplinary committee to look into the allegations of
184misconduct and this to be in line with the concept of "natural justice". Given the
185circumstances of the present application there is no ground to prohibit or restrain
186the respondent from the course embarked upon.
187(2) The contention that the proposed composition of the committee might be
188biased is misconceived. As rightly pointed out by learned Counsel for the
189respondent such an objection cannot be invoked at this stage.”
190In the present case, learned Counsel for the respondent further argued that the applicant
191has a right of appeal against a decision of the Board under section 67 of the Competition Act
1922008. There are also other remedies available to her before the Industrial Court which is
193statutorily vested with exclusive jurisdiction to hear such cases.
194I can only concur with the pertinent remarks made by Mrs Justice G. Jugessur – Manna
195in the case of Bhoolah v ICTA [2016 SCJ 414]:
196“I fail to understand the fear and apprehension of the applicant to appear before
197a disciplinary committee where he will have the opportunity to be represented by
198counsel, to cross-examine witnesses called by the respondent, to call witness
199and produce documents in his favour and to depose, present his defence and
200clear his name. The applicant cannot pre-empt the findings of the disciplinary
201committee and the decision of the respondent. The Employment Rights Act
202provides for adequate safeguard to an employee who is facing disciplinary
203proceedings. Other than mere apprehension, the applicant has not averred any
204infringement of the provisions of the Employment Rights Act by the respondent.
205However, the applicant can always have recourse to the proper forum in case he
206is dissatisfied with the decision of the respondent or when his rights have been
207infringed during or after the disciplinary proceeding.’’
208Turning to the issue of damages the principle was reiterated in the case of Sujeeun B &
209Anor v Issur C & Anor [2017 SCJ 248] and Kurreemun v C.E.B [2018 SCJ 27]:
210“By the undertaking the applicant is required to compensate the respondent for
211any loss incurred by the defendant during the currency of the injunction if it later
212appears following the outcome of a substantive case that the injunction was
213wrongly granted. Its purpose is to provide a safeguard for the defendant who
214may be unjustifiably prevented from doing something it was entitled to do.
215[Blackstone‘s Civil Practice 2011 Edition Para 37.24’]’’
216It is the case for the applicant that she is most unlikely to recover damages from the
217respondent as the issue to be thrashed out only relates to the composition of the disciplinary
218committee and not to the hearing of the committee and the balance of convenience should tilt in
219favour of the applicant who reckons 10 years of service with the respondent.
220The Court averred in the case of Balmick Manoj Rajkamal Hanoomanjee v Sun
221Resorts Ltd [2011 SCJ 1] that “the sanction for any eventual breach or unjustified dismissal of
222the employee would be the different severance allowances provided for the Act or in damages.”
223
2247
225It is also held that “those in management of an enterprise know how best to run it and must be
226at liberty to take any disciplinary action they deem fit to take when they are in presence of facts
227prima facie showing that an employee has committed a misconduct.”
228From the affidavit evidence on record, I fail to see that applicant could not be adequately
229compensated in damages. On the contrary the balance of convenience tilts in favour of the
230respondent where if prevented for a longer period to keep on hold the disciplinary committee far
231outweighs that which would be caused to the applicant should it eventually be held in the main
232case that the disciplinary committee was wrongly instituted in view of the composition of the
233Board.
234The last preliminary objection relates to the fact that there is no triable issue as applicant
235holds no legal right capable of being enforced by an injunction.
236The distinctiveness of the jurisdiction of the “juge des référés” from that of the jurisdiction
237of the Judge in Chambers to grant interlocutory relief has been highlighted by the Judicial
238Committee of the Privy Council in Gujadhur v Gujadhur [2006 PRV 51]:
239For the applicant to succeed under Article 806 of the Code de Procédure Civile, I
240have to be satisfied that –
241(a) the applicant has a clear legal right;
242(b) there is an urgency which calls for the granting of the order prayed for at this
243stage; and
244(c) the respondent has not raised a serious and bona fide defence.
245The reason why applicant is seeking an interim injunction as averred in her affidavit
246dated the 10 October 2019 at paragraph 23 is mainly because “respondent and co-respondents
247are acting in defiance of the law and her employment rights and her right to a fair hearing
248requires urgent protection against the aforesaid acts and doings of the respondent and co-
249respondents.”
250The remedy which applicant is seeking is to prevent an administrative body to perform
251its duties under the law. This is clearly outside my jurisdiction as a Judge sitting in Chambers.
252As a matter of fact, no element of public law has been disclosed.
253In Le Repaire Resort Ltee v Rodrigues Regional Assembly [2012 SCJ 113] (cited
254with approval in Mungroo v Ministry of Agro Industry [2014 SCJ 304]) Y K J Yeung Sik Yuen
255Chief Justice and K P Matadeen Senior Puisne Judge held the following:
256
2578
258“In the present case, the issue is essentially one of contract between two parties
259and the interpretation of contractual terms which had been put in writing and
260signed by the parties. That one of the parties to the contract, the respondent, is
261a public body, does not create a public law element in the resolution mechanism
262of any dispute it may have with the applicant.’’
263The action of the applicant is procedurally wrong and is indeed a disguised application
264for a judicial review which cannot be allowed to succeed (vide-TKM Consultants Ltd v The
265Mauritius Qualifications Authority & Ors [2007 SCJ 207] “the affidavits show that the
266applicant is seeking in effect to challenge an administrative decision of the first respondent
267whose powers and duties are set out in the Act. The application is accordingly a disguised form
268of judicial review which is not within the jurisdiction of the Judge in Chambers, vide-(Le Petit
269Morne Ltee v Town and Country Planning Board [1998 SCJ 142] and K. S. Dhunnoo v The
270Private Secondary School Authority [2002 SCJ 186]”.
271For the reasons given above, the application is consequently set aside, with costs.

Headnote

Facts

The applicant was employed as a Confidential Personal Assistant by the Competition Commission since 4 January 2010. On 23 August 2019, she was suspended under section 38(2)(a)(iii) of the Employment Rights Act for allegedly failing to record receipt of an envelope containing a tender proposal on 15 August 2019 and to inform superiors of its arrival, when the tender's closing date was that same day. The applicant did not deliver the envelope until the afternoon of 16 August 2019. A disciplinary committee hearing was scheduled for 17 October 2019. The applicant applied for an interim injunction to restrain the disciplinary proceedings, challenging the composition of the committee on grounds of bias and denial of natural justice.
