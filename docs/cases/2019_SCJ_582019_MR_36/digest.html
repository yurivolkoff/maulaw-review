78The Law
79The duty of confidentiality of a banker is sacrosanct and of vital importance for the
80stability of the financial system and for public confidence in the banking sector.
81The following extract gives an insight into this issue –
82“Just as the English common law has laid down that there is an implied
83term of confidentiality in the contractual relationship between a banker and his
84customer, we find a similar approach in French law, by which we must be
85governed in this matter.
86Dans l'exercice de sa profession, le banquier recueille de multiples
87renseignements sur les affaires de ses clients ou de tiers et parfois même
88il a connaissance de faits personnels ou familiaux. Il est unanimement
89admis que le banquier est tenu d'un devoir de discrétion sanctionné par
90une responsabilité civile. (Droit de la Banque, Cavaldaet Stouffiet, at p
91393).
92But that is not all and reference must be made to section 300 of the
93Criminal Code which reads as follows -
94
954
96Les médecins, chirurgiens, ainsi que les pharmaciens, les sages-femmes
97et toutes autres personnes dépositaires, par état ou professions des
98secrets qu'on leur confie, qui, hors le cas où la loi les oblige à se porter
99dénonciateurs, auront révélé ces secrets seront punis . . .
100It is generally agreed that a banker is one of those "autres personnes
101dépositaires ... des secrets" (See op cit at page 397). Of course while doctors,
102priests and lawyers stand in a separate category, the authors are agreed that
103bankers, for example, are more liable to be compelled to reveal the secrets they
104hold when the public interest, and particularly the detection of crime, so require.”
105(Director of Public Prosecutions v. Indian Ocean International Bank [1987
106MR 21])
107Legislation has since intervened by dealing expressly with the situation where in the
108public interest and the detection of crime, the disclosure of information held by a bank, is
109required. Any such information may only be obtained by court order and in compliance with the
110stringent conditions laid down in “The Banking Act” (“The Act”).
111Section 64 of The Act starts first by imposing a strict duty of confidentiality and non-
112disclosure of any information relating to the affairs of any of the banks’ customers.
113Section 64(2) provides the following in that connection:
114“64. Confidentiality
115(2) Except for the purpose of the performance of his duties or the exercise
116of his functions under the banking laws or as directed in writing by the
117central bank, no person referred to in subsection (1) shall, during or after
118his relationship with the financial institution, disclose directly or indirectly
119to any person any information relating to the affairs of any of its
120customers including any deposits, borrowings or transactions or other
121personal, financial or business affairs, without the prior written consent of
122the customer or his personal representative.”
123Furthermore to reinforce the implementation of that duty of confidentiality, Section 64(1)
124creates an obligation for officers of a financial institution including a bank to take an oath of
125confidentiality –
126
1275
128“64. Confidentiality
129(1) (a) Subject to this Act, every person, including a service provider who
130by virtue of his professional relationship with a financial institution, has
131access to the books, accounts, records, financial statements or other
132documents, whether electronically or otherwise, of a financial institution
133shall –
134(i) in the case of a director or senior officer, take an oath of
135confidentiality in the form set out in the First Schedule;
136(ii) in the case of a director or service provider who is a non-
137resident, take an oath of confidentiality before the
138competent court or authority in the country of residence of
139the director or service provider, in such form as the central
140bank may approve; or
141(iii) in any other case, make a declaration of confidentiality
142before the chief executive officer or deputy chief executive
143officer of the financial institution in the form set out in the
144Second Schedule,
145before he begins to perform any duties under the banking laws.”
146Section 64(16) goes on to provide that Section 64 of The Act shall in the event of any
147conflict or inconsistency with any other Act prevail over any such enactment, subject to some
148statutory exceptions –
149“64. Confidentiality
150(16) In the event of any conflict or inconsistency between any provision
151of this section and the provisions of any other enactment, other than the
152Bank of Mauritius Act 2004, section 11(5)(a) of the Commissions of
153Inquiry Act, the Financial Intelligence and Anti-money Laundering Act
1542002 and sections 123, 123D and 124 of the Income Tax Act and the
155Mutual Assistance in Criminal and Related Matters Act 2003, the
156provisions of this section shall prevail.”
157A contravention of Section 64 constitutes an offence which is punishable by severe
158penalties including a fine not exceeding Rs 1M and a term of imprisonment not exceeding five
159years [vide Section 97(20)].

162The above provisions of the law have highlighted the strict nature of a bank’s obligation
163not to disclose information pertaining to its customers’ affairs. This can only be done in
164situations which fall within the purview of statutory exceptions and subject to the fulfillment of all
165the statutory conditions.
166It is apposite to consider the circumstances in which a bank is entitled to disclose
167information relating to its customers’ affairs.
168We need first to consider two provisions in “The Banking Act” i.e. Section 60(3) and
169Section 64(10) of The Act.
170Section 60(3) deals with entries in a banker’s book. It provides that –
171“A Judge in Chambers or any court may, on the application of any party
172to legal proceedings, order that such party be permitted to obtain copies
173of any entry in a banker's book where such entry is material to the
174proceedings.” (Emphasis added)
175Section 60(5) gives the following definition of “banker’s books”:
176“For the purposes of this section, "banker's books" includes ledgers, day
177books, cash books, account books, records, financial statements or other
178documents used in the ordinary course of business of a bank, whether all
179these are in written form or are kept on microfilm, magnetic tape, or any
180other form of mechanical or electronic data retrieval mechanism.”
181The next provision in The Banking Act is Section 64(10) under which the Judge in
182Chambers may make an order of disclosure. The section provides that:
18364(10) “The Judge in Chambers shall not make an order of disclosure unless he is
184satisfied that –
185(a) the applicant is acting in the discharge of his or its duties;
186(b) the information is material to any civil or criminal proceedings,
187whether pending or contemplated or is required for the purpose of any
188enquiry into or relating to the trafficking of narcotics and dangerous drugs,

191arms trafficking, offences related to terrorism under the Prevention of
192Terrorism Act 2002 or money laundering under the Financial Intelligence

194(c) the disclosure is otherwise necessary, in all the circumstances.”

19764(9) “The Director-General under the Prevention of Corruption Act 2002,

237I do not agree with the above arguments regarding the application of Section 64(10). In
238my view Section 64(10) of The Act which has been invoked in support of this application, cannot
239be read in isolation, it has to be read contextually within Section 64.
240Section 64 is a comprehensive piece of legislation designed by virtue of its structure and
241a scheme, to deal holistically with the issue of confidentiality. The section sets out in detail the
242limited circumstances and the conditions subject to which, there may be a disclosure of any
243information relating to the affairs of a bank customer.
244The persons who are empowered to make an application to the Judge in Chambers for
245an order of disclosure under Section 64, are listed in Section 64(9) which immediately precedes
246Section 64(10). Section 64(10) goes on to provide the conditions which must be satisfied before
247the Judge in Chambers can make an order of disclosure pursuant to Section 64.
248The Judge in Chambers may therefore issue an order for disclosure pursuant to Section
24964(10) where the Judge is satisfied that the applicant is acting in his or its duties and the
250conditions set out under either Section 64(10)(b) or Section 64(10)(c), are satisfied and then
251only, upon an application by an “applicant” who must be one of the designated authorities listed
252in Section 64(9). This is made explicit by the use of the word or which has been inserted only in
253between (b) and (c).

257Section 64(10) of The Act.
258Indeed the relevance and application of Section 64(10) in the present circumstances,
259has already been pronounced upon by the Court of Civil Appeal, in connection with a previous
260application lodged by the applicant himself by way of motion before the Bankruptcy Division, in
261respect of the very same matter and involving the very same issue i.e. the communication of

265I had set aside the application on the ground that it was misconceived and could not be

268(i) Applicants are not parties to “legal proceedings” as contemplated
269by Section 60(3) of The Act;
270(ii) The application concerns cheques and does not relate to any
271“entry” in a “banker’s book” within the meaning of Section 60(3);
272(iii) The application does not fall within the ambit of Section 64(10)
273since the applicants are not among the persons or authorities who
274are entitled to make an application for an order of disclosure albeit
275to the Judge in Chambers under Section 64(10)
276The Appellate Court did not disturb the decision and maintained the judgment so that the
277issue regarding the application of Section 64(10) in the present circumstances, is not only
278misconceived and erroneous in principle, but it is also a matter which is “res judicata”. Section
27964(10) is accordingly of no avail to the applicant.

281relating to discovery laid down in the English case of Norwich Pharmacal (supra) and which
282have been applied in England in certain applications for disclosure of information from banking
283institutions.

286It is first useful to refer to the principles established under the common law with respect
287to matters relating to the bank/customer relationship. It has been held by the Court of Appeal in
288the case of Tournier v. National Provincial and Union Bank of England [1924] 1 K.B. 461,
289that a duty of confidentiality was to be implied into the contract between banker and customer
290and that such duty extends beyond the termination of the relationship between them.
291However the duty is not absolute and is subject to the following qualifications:
292(a) where disclosure is under compulsion by law;
293(b) where there is a duty to the public to disclose;
294(c) where the interests of the bank require disclosure; and
295(d) where the customer impliedly or expressly consents to disclosure.
296(Tournier (supra) per Bankes LJ at page 473)
297The law has since evolved and a disclosure order may be obtained at common law
298under the principles set out in the case of Norwich Pharmacal (supra). The tenor and purport
299of the Norwich Pharmacal order, is explained in the following extract –
300A Norwich Pharmacal order may require a person, such as a bank, to
301disclose information to a third party in particular circumstances, equating
302broadly to the “interest of justice”.
303Such an order for disclosure by a bank may be made when the bank has
304innocently become involved in the wrongful acts of another and
305information is urgently required by the victim of the wrongful act (and use
306of a witness summons will not provide sufficient or adequate relief and
307assistance).”
308(Banking Litigation, Fourth Edition (2017), by Charles Hewetson and
309Gregory Mitshell QC - Sweet & Maxwell at page 335)
310The court in Norwich Pharmacal (supra) set out guidelines for the exercise of a
311Judge’s discretion to allow discovery of information in the course of civil proceedings and
312prescribed the conditions necessary for the issue of such an order.
313The three conditions to be satisfied for the court to exercise the power to order Norwich
314Pharmacal relief are as follows:
315(i) a wrong must have been carried out, or arguably carried out, by
316an ultimate wrongdoer;
317
31811
319The standard of proof is that of a good arguable case as in freezing injunction cases:
320see Ramilos Trading Limited v. Buyanovsky [2016] EWHC 3175 at [27]. In Norwich
321Pharmacal itself the wrong was a tort. However, any type of wrong may be sufficient,
322including breach of trust or other equitable wrong and fraudulent payments: see
323Matthews and Malek, Disclosure (5th edn) at para 3.05
324(ii) there must be the need for an order to enable action to be brought
325against the ultimate wrongdoer;
326Ramilos at [24]:
327The second condition for relief is that the disclosure sought must be necessary in order
328to enable the applicant to bring legal proceedings or seek other legitimate redress for the
329wrongdoing and in considering the question of necessity, the cases emphasise the need
330for flexibility and discretion. …
331(iii) the person against whom the order is sought must: (a) be mixed
332up in so to have facilitated the wrongdoing; and (b) be able or
333likely to be able to provide the information necessary to enable the
334ultimate wrongdoer to be sued.
335Finally, even if the three threshold requirements are satisfied, the court has a
336discretion as to whether to make the order …”
337[Miles Smith Broking Limited v Barclays Bank Plc [2017] EWHC 3338 (Ch)]
338It is also useful to refer to the comments made by Lord Bingham In the case of
339President of the State of Equatorial Guinea v Royal Bank of Scotland International,
340Equatorial Guinea v Royal Bank of Scotland International [2006] UKPC 7 to the effect that
341where the applicant has “no straightforward or available, or any, means of finding out, Norwich
342Pharmacal relief is in principle available if the other conditions of obtaining relief are met”. Lord
343Bingham further went on to state that:
344“whether it is said that it must be just and convenient in the interests of
345justice to grant relief, or that relief should only be granted if it is necessary
346in the interests of justice to grant it, makes little or no difference of
347substance” …”
348(Banking Litigation (supra) at page 336)
349It is necessary to determine whether the common law principles established in Norwich
350Pharmacal can be resorted to by our courts and if so, in what circumstances.
351
35212
353As already stated above The Banking Act has provided for specific instances wherein
354disclosure is permissible namely under Section 60(3) and Section 64(10). The questions that
355arise at this juncture are as follows:
356- Is The Banking Act a comprehensive statutory regime which covers
357exhaustively the whole question of confidentiality so that it precludes
358the application of any alternative common law remedy such as the
359Norwich Pharmacal Relief?
360- Has Parliament legislated to delimit the only situations wherein
361disclosure of confidential information in a banker’s possession, is
362permissible?
363- Would the application of the Norwich Pharmacal principles be
364tantamount to circumventing the intention of Parliament and would it
365be incompatible with or in violation of, the statutory scheme under The
366Banking Act?
367This will no doubt require a close examination of the relevant sections of The Banking
368Act in order to determine whether a disclosure of such confidential information may only be
369obtained via the statutory channel prescribed under The Act.
370Although reference to the Norwich Pharmacal principles has been made in a few cases
371in Mauritius, the question whether the common law principles enunciated in that case would
372apply in view of the provisions of The Banking Act, has never been frontally addressed. In
373Drouin J.M & Ors v Bank of Baroda [2008 SCJ 304] the applicants had invested substantial
374amounts of money in an immoveable project with one Mr. Gerard Rochecouste who had already
375passed away at the time of the application.
376The applicants made an application against several banks to the Judge in Chambers
377under Section 64 of The Banking Act for disclosure of accounts held at the banks as well as
378transactions carried out by late G. Rochecouste and his partner, a Chinese national who had
379already fled the country.
380The purpose of the order was to enable the applicants to trace the money which they
381had handed over to G. Rochecouste, and to ascertain whether it had been deposited in any
382bank account.
383
38413
385The stand of the banks was that it would abide by the decision of the Judge, the Judge
386granted the application. The Judge referred to the provisions of The Banking Act, he also
387referred to the case of Norwich Pharmacal & Co. and Others and Customs and Excise
388Commissioners [1974] A.C. 133 and Bankers Trust Co v. Shapira and Ors CA [1980] 3 All
389ER 352 of his “own accord” as “cases which might be enlightening to this entirely new area of
390the law”.
391After citing an extract from the judgment of Norwich Pharmacal, the Judge went on to
392refer to Lord Dennings’ remark in Bankers Trust Co (supra) to the effect that “this new
393jurisdiction must, of course, be exercised carefully”.
394In the subsequent case of Li Soop Hon Li Tung Sang & Co. Ltd v. Barclays Bank
395PLC & Ors [2014 SCJ 242] the applicant had applied to the Judge in Chambers for an order of
396disclosure of information in respect of bank accounts held by a former employee of the applicant
397on the basis that the said employee had misappropriated funds belonging to the applicant and
398credited them in his bank accounts. The application was made pursuant to Sections 64(3)(g)(h)
399and (10) of The Act, the Judge granted the application.
400In his judgment the Judge referred to the principles governing disclosure of information
401from third parties enunciated in the case of Norwich Pharmacal (supra) and also to Section 64
402of The Banking Act. However the precise legal basis on which the application was granted has
403not been set out in the judgment nor has it been expressly stated that the order was granted
404following an application of the Norwich Pharmacal principles.
405Although the decision in Norwich Pharmacal provides some useful insight as to the
406approach which the court would adopt for allowing discovery of information in the course of civil
407proceedings, the present matter stands governed essentially by the express and detailed
408provisions of Section 64 of The Act which section, has been invoked in support of the
409application. In any case, the Norwich Pharmacal order is not warranted in this case.
410The conditions required for the issue of a Norwich Pharmacal order, are not here
411satisfied inasmuch as the applicant already has the information which he is purportedly seeking
412to obtain by a court order. He has precise information regarding the number, amount, date and
413recipients of all the cheques in lite and has given precise details about these cheques both in
414
41514
416the present application and in his plaint with summons. He has at this stage all the information
417that is required for him to proceed with his case. As such it cannot be said that the applicant
418“has no straightforward or available or any means of finding out” the information required.
419What the applicant is in effect seeking is to obtain the cheques themselves for the
420purpose which he has stated i.e. so as to give a declaration to the Police and for production at
421the hearing of the case which he has lodged against the co-respondent and Khaled and Bahim
422Cheddee.
423The evidence does not indicate that there is any impediment for the applicant to give any
424declaration against Khaled and Bahim Chedee with the information available to him which would
425enable the Police if necessary, to seek an order for disclosure under Section 64(9) and Section
42664(10) of The Banking Act.
427In so far as the production of the cheques at the trial is concerned, it is open for the
428applicant to summon the respondents who are compellable witnesses to produce the cheques
429in lite subject to the trial court ruling that these cheques are relevant and admissible.
430The applicant has in fact also prayed for an order that the respondents be authorised
431and ordered:
432“to depose as to and produce all such cheques disclosed in at paragraph
43354 when called as witnesses in the main case bearing reference
434SC/COM/PWS/01428/2015 which is pending before the Commercial
435Division of the Supreme Court.”
436In his submissions counsel for the applicant has stated that the “applicant is simply
437praying that respondents should be ordered to produce all the cheques referred to when called
438as witnesses in the main case. Therefore, it is only when they are summoned as witnesses that
439they will have to come and give evidence. Moreover, the summons to the respondents will
440order them to come as witnesses to depose as witnesses and to produce the cheques under
441reference”.
442

444Counsel for the co-respondent has submitted that this prayer is outside the jurisdiction of
445the Judge in Chambers. Counsel pointed out there is as at now, a case pending before the
446Supreme Court in this matter. The evidence to be adduced before that court is a matter for that
447court itself to decide and not the Judge in Chambers. Counsel further added that at the hearing
448before the Supreme Court, the co-respondent will probably take a legal issue with respect to the
449cause of action which is grounded in tort whereas the relationship existing between the parties,
450is a contractual one. According to counsel a litigant cannot obtain “disclosure of somebody
451else’s personal banking information from the Judge in Chambers on the sole ground that
452proceedings have been lodged against that person, when the said action is totally devoid of
453merits”.
454I agree with the submissions of counsel for the co-respondent that the order prayed for,
455does not fall within the jurisdictional powers of the Judge in Chambers. This issue has been
456addressed by the Court of Civil Appeal sitting on appeal upon a decision of the Judge in
457Chambers setting aside an application for disclosure made pursuant to Section 64(3)(h) of The
458Act. The court made it clear that –
459“…While the question of admissibility of documents as evidence is one for
460the trial Court, the Judge in Chambers clearly has jurisdiction to
461determine the question as to whether the documents can be disclosed to
462the applicant for the latter to use at the trial, if so minded and if allowed to
463do so by the trial Court.” (Nundoosingh J. v Standard Bank (Mauritius)
464Limited [2018 SCJ 122]) (Emphasis added)
465Section 64 of The Act gives jurisdiction to the Judge in Chambers to make disclosure
466orders subject to the requisite conditions being satisfied; it does not provide for the issue of
467orders compelling banks to depone and produce confidential information in their possession, in
468a pending trial. The banks are compellable witnesses who may be summoned at the trial to
469produce the information sought and such information may be disclosed if the court so allows. It
470is a matter which is eminently within the discretion of the trial court to determine whether the
471information may be disclosed.
472

474The duty of confidentiality is not applicable in such circumstances. In that connection
475Section 64(3)(h) of The Act provides that –
476“The duty of confidentiality imposed under this section shall not apply
477where […]
478[h] any person referred to in subsection [1] is summoned to
479appear before a Court or Judge in Mauritius and the Court or the
480Judge orders the disclosure of the information.” (Emphasis added)
481Counsel for the applicant has already intimated that he will call the respondents as
482witnesses at the trial and the question of production and admissibility of the cheques as
483evidence before the trial court, is a matter for that court alone to decide. It will be for the court to
484assess the relevance of the cheques and whether in the circumstances it is necessary that the
485confidentiality of the information be lifted. The Judge in Chambers cannot be invoked as a
486parallel jurisdiction with that of the court before which the main case is pending and which has
487the jurisdiction to pronounce upon the admissibility and production of the cheques.
488For all the above reasons I set aside the application. With costs, and I certify as to

Headnote

Facts

The applicant invested in a construction project with Maik Construction Ltd under a profit-sharing agreement. During the project, the applicant contributed additional funds to cover payments to suppliers and subcontractors because the principal was remiss in payment. The applicant claims the co-respondent's directors fraudulently misappropriated his share of profits and made numerous dubious payments unrelated to the project. Seeking to lodge a police complaint and gather evidence for a pending commercial case, the applicant applied to the Judge in Chambers for certified copies of cheques evidencing the dubious payments, relying on both Section 64(10) of The Banking Act and common law Norwich Pharmacal relief.

Held

  1. A private litigant is not entitled to apply for disclosure of bank customer information under Section 64(10) of The Banking Act, as that provision restricts applications to the designated authorities listed in Section 64(9), namely the Director-General under the Prevention of Corruption Act 2002, the Chief Executive of the Financial Services Commission, the Commissioner of Police, the Director-General of the Mauritius Revenue Authority, and the Enforcement Authority under the Asset Recovery Act 2011.

    L237-L252 — I do not agree with the above arguments regarding the application of Section 64(10). In my view Section 64(10) of The Act which has been invoked in support of this application, cannot be read in isolation, it has to be read contextually wi…L256-L257 — The applicant clearly does not fulfill the criteria laid down under Section 64(9) and Section 64(10) of The Act.

  2. A previous dismissal of a similar application by the applicant is res judicata and precludes relitigation of whether Section 64(10) applies to the applicant, the issue having been considered and upheld by the Court of Civil Appeal.

    L258-L279 — Indeed the relevance and application of Section 64(10) in the present circumstances, has already been pronounced upon by the Court of Civil Appeal, in connection with a previous application lodged by the applicant himself by way of motion …

  3. Norwich Pharmacal relief is not available where the applicant already possesses all the material information necessary to pursue legal proceedings, including cheque numbers, amounts, dates, and recipients, even if the physical cheques remain in the banks' possession.

    L410-L426 — The conditions required for the issue of a Norwich Pharmacal order, are not here satisfied inasmuch as the applicant already has the information which he is purportedly seeking to obtain by a court order. He has precise information regardi…

  4. The Judge in Chambers lacks jurisdiction to order banks to depone and produce confidential information at trial; that matter lies solely within the discretion of the trial court, which may allow production when compellable witnesses are summoned and the court determines the information is relevant and admissible.

    L454-L471 — I agree with the submissions of counsel for the co-respondent that the order prayed for, does not fall within the jurisdictional powers of the Judge in Chambers. This issue has been addressed by the Court of Civil Appeal sitting on appeal …

Disposition

Application is set aside with costs and the court certifies as to counsel.

Quotable holding

A private litigant cannot obtain an order for disclosure of bank customer information under Section 64(10) of The Banking Act, as that section restricts applications to the designated public authorities listed in Section 64(9).
