11Introduction
121. The Applicant stands charged with the offences of drug dealing: delivering
13heroin in breach of sections 30(1)(d)(ii) and 47(5)(a) of the Dangerous Drugs Act
14under Count I, drug dealing: possession of synthetic drugs for the purpose
15of selling in breach of sections 30(1)(f)(ii) and 47(5)(a) of the Dangerous Drugs
16Act under Count II and money laundering in breach of sections 3(1)(b), 6, 7 and
178(1)(a) of the Financial Intelligence and Anti Money Laundering Act under Count
18III. He has been on remand since the 10th July 2020 and has moved to be
19admitted to bail.

22two doses of suspected heroin to a co-Accused, which the latter sold to
23undercover police officers. The Police also secured Rs 600, suspected to be the
24proceeds of drugs, from the Applicant, and during a search at his place, 5.71
25grams of suspected synthetic cannabinoids was also secured. The total value of
26the drugs secured is Rs 29,500 and the Applicant has confessed to possession
27only.

313. PS Seedam, of ADSU Rose-Belle, has been deputed by the Commissioner of

33(i) Risk of reoffending

35business and taking into account that the Applicant is borne on record for six drug
36related offences, including one for drug dealing (Doc X) and is on bail for a

44The Law
457. I bear in mind the sacrosanct principle of presumption of innocence and the
46Constitutional rights of the Applicant to enjoy freedom and liberty.
478. Section 3 of the Bail Act provides that “Notwithstanding any other enactment
48and subject to section 4, every defendant or detainee shall be entitled to be
49released on bail.”
509. Section 4 (1) of the Bail Act reads as follows:
51A court may refuse to release a defendant or detainee on bail where –
52(a) it is satisfied that there is reasonable ground to believe that the defendant or
53detainee, if released, is likely to –
542 | P a ge
55
56(i) fail to surrender to custody or to appear before a Court as and when
57required;
58(ii) commit an offence, other than an offence punishable only by a fine;
59(iii) interfere with witnesses, tamper with evidence or otherwise obstruct
60the course of justice, in relation to him or to any other person;
61(b) it is satisfied that the defendant or detainee should be kept in custody –
62(i) for his own protection;
63(ii) in the case of a minor, for his own welfare; or
64(iii) for the preservation of public order;
65(c) the defendant or detainee, having been released on bail, has –
66(i) committed an act referred to in paragraph (a); or
67(ii) breached any other condition imposed on him for his release.
68(d) the defendant or detainee is charged or is likely to be charged with a serious
69offence;
70(e) there is reasonable ground for believing that the defendant or detainee has –
71(i) given false or misleading information regarding his names or address;
72or
73(ii) no fixed place of abode;
74(f) a detainee has failed to comply with section 12 (2).
7510. Section 4(2) of the Bail Act provides that:

78In considering whether or not to refuse bail on any ground mentioned in
79subsection (1), the Court shall decide the matter by weighing the interests of
80society against the right of the defendant or detainee to his liberty and the
81prejudice he is likely to suffer if he is detained in custody, taking into account
82every consideration which, in its opinion, is relevant, including –
83(a) the period for which the defendant or detainee has already been in custody
84since his arrest;
85(b) the nature and gravity of the offence with which the defendant or detainee is
86or is likely to be charged and the nature and gravity of the penalty which may
87be imposed on him;
88(c) the character, association, means, community ties and antecedents of the
89defendant or detainee, including any non-compliance with any condition
90imposed for his release on bail with respect to any other offence; and
91(d) the nature of the evidence available with regard to the offence with which the
92defendant is charged.
9311. The rationale as to bail was clearly set out in the authority of Maloupe v The
94District Magistrate of Grand Port [2000] SCJ 233, where it was held that :
95“The rationale of the law of bail at pre-trial stage is, accordingly, that a person
96should normally be released on bail if the imposition of the conditions reduces
97the risks referred to above – i.e. risk of absconding, risk to the administration of
98justice, risk to society – to such an extent that they become negligible having
99regard to the weight which the presumption of innocence should carry in the
100balance When the imposition of the above conditions is considered to be unlikely
101to make any of the above risks negligible, then bail is to be refused.”
10212. I bear in mind that the right balance has to be struck between the constitutional
103right of the Applicant to enjoy freedom and the interest of society at large.
10413. It was held in Deelchand v The Director of Public Prosecutions and Others
105(2005) SCJ 215 that “In all countries where human rights are respected, the
106function of the law of bail is likely to be the same, being to reconcile, as stated in
1074 | P a ge
108
109Labonne v The D.P.P (supra, at para 2.2.), “on the one hand the need to
110safeguard the necessary respect for the liberty of the citizen viewed in the
111context of the presumption of innocence and, on the other hand, the need to
112ensure that society and the administration of justice are reasonably protected
113against serious risks which might materialise in the event that the detainee is
114really a criminal which he is suspected to be.”
11514. In Islam v The Senior District Magistrate Grand Port District Court [2006]
116SCJ 282 at para 37, it was held that “Pre-trial bail is not a form of punishment to
117any individual. It is a form of partial and temporary restriction of his liberty with a
118view to disabling him against antisocial behavior.”
119Finding
12015. In relation to the ground of objection, that is the risk of re-offending,
121considering the value of drugs involved in the present case, the nature of the
122evidence against the Applicant, and drug dealing being a lucrative business, I
123find that there is indeed a high risk that the Applicant might indulge anew in such
124transactions should he be released on bail. Docs A and X show that the latter
125has the propensity to commit the offence at hand, the more so since he has one
126previous conviction for drug dealing and is on bail for a drug dealing offence.
127The present ground therefore succeeds after considering the following
128authorities:
129(a) In Deelchand v The Director of Public Prosecutions & Ors [2005] SCJ
130215 at para 5.8, it was held that :
131“The sentence expected in case of conviction is also a relevant consideration
132because of the greater risk that the offender may think he has nothing to lose
133by offending whilst on bail. The extent to which the offences which the
134applicant is suspected to have committed are lucrative should also be
135considered as the temptation, in case the applicant is guilty, that he may wish
136to make as much money as possible whilst on bail, is likely to be greater.”
137(b) In Islam (Supra) at para 32, it was held that:
138“Thus, somebody who is admitted to bail, who has been able to purchase –
139so to speak - his freedom by some monetary condition, walks out of the court-
140room, free from any type of effective control over his movements or his
1415 | P a ge
142
143activities other than presumably reporting to the nearest police station twice a
144day. In between, where he goes, under whose influence he falls, with whom
145he associates himself to pursue what design is left to himself.”
146(c) In Hossen v District Magistrate of Port Louis [1993] MR 9, it was held that :
147“Everyone knows that the consumption of certain drugs, like any other vice
148one may think of, is never likely to disappear completely from the face of the
149planet and that, at times, it may not be reasonable to insist on the detention of
150suspects of a certain type. But when, on the contrary, we are faced with a
151proliferation of drug consumption, or a resurgence of this scourge which can
152only result in the corruption and degradation of the country’s youth in
153particular, then the Courts have the duty and the responsibility to protect the
154public against every person who is involved in any activity that is likely to
155facilitate or encourage the drug trade”.
15616. Having found that the ground of objection has been substantiated, this Court has
157an inherent duty to assess whether conditions can be imposed to adequately
158curb the risks identified, so as to render such risks negligible as expounded in
159the case of Maloupe (Supra). In Deelchand (Supra), it was held that “Security
160appears applicable to all the risks as it may be an incentive to the applicant to
161appear for trial, to behave whilst on bail and to refrain from interfering with
162witnesses, or tampering with evidence.”
16317. In the case of Rangasamy M.N. v The D.P.P & Anor 2005 SCJ 249, the Court
164stated the following:
165“We consider that judicial officers in Mauritius who have first-hand knowledge of
166the prevailing local conditions regarding law and order and organized crime
167should have a margin of appreciation in exercising their discretion and deciding
168on the need for a detainee to be admitted to bail, taking into account all the
169public interest grounds for refusing bail listed in section 4 of the Act”.
17018. The circumstances of the present case are such that no conditions can be
171imposed to reduce the risks that have been identified. As such, this Court
172declines to exercise its discretion in favour of the Applicant and finds that his
173continued detention is warranted. The present application is, therefore, set
174aside.
