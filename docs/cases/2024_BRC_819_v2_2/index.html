12RULING
13Applicant stands provisionally charged with the offence of sequestration in breach of section 258(1) of
14the Criminal Code.
15Applicant applied for bail. He was not assisted by Counsel. Respondent objected to the bail application on
16the ground that Applicant may reoffend.
17Section 4(1)(a)(ii) of the Bail Act 1999 (Act 32/1999) [“the Bail Act”] provides that a Court may refuse
18to release a defendant or a detainee on bail where it is satisfied that there is reasonable ground for
19believing that the defendant or detainee, if released, is likely to commit an offence, other than an offence
20punishable only by a fine.
21The Supreme Court in Deelchand v The Director of Public Prosecutions and others [2005 SCJ 215]
22underlined that the risk of offending must be a real one and there must be adequate reasons to explain its
23existence. The Supreme Court also cited with approval the case of Clooth v Belgium [1991] ECHR 71
24(12 December 1991) where it was said that the danger of a serious offence being committed by an
25applicant whilst on bail should be a plausible one.
26In the case at hand, it transpires from the testimony of the enquiry officer, Sergeant Toolsee, that
27Applicant confessed having committed the offence. As such, the nature of the evidence against him is
28strong.
29Original District Court (BR) CN: 173/24 – p.1
30
31In addition, a PF 15 and a certificate from the Mauritius Police Force were produced to show that
32Applicant is borne on record. Same were marked as Doc X and Doc PPU. It appears from the
33documentary evidence that Applicant was sentence to imprisonment almost every year between 2014 and
342023, and despite being on bail, he is again provisionally charged with another offence. Hence, the risk of
35offending is plausible.
36The rationale of the law of bail at pre-trial stage is explained in Maloupe v District Magistrate of Grand
37Port i.p.o. Director of Public Prosecutions [2000 SCJ 223]. The Supreme Court observed that a person
38should normally be released on bail if the imposition of the conditions reduces the risk of absconding, risk
39to the administration of justice, risk to society to such an extent that they become negligible having regard
40to the weight which the presumption of innocence should carry in the balance; and when the imposition of
41the conditions is considered to be unlikely to make any of the risks negligible, then bail is to be refused.
42Here, it is apposite to note from the Doc X and Doc PPU that Applicant has a propensity to commit
43offences which are not punishable by fine only. At this stage, the Court cannot entertain the bail
44application because the imposition of conditions is unlikely to make the risk of offending negligible
45having regard to the weight which the presumption of innocence would carry in the balance.
46However, the Court notes that Applicant is held in custody since 04.02.2024, and there is no indication
47when exactly the main case will be lodged.
48In Hossen v District Magistrate of Port Louis [1993 MR 9], the Supreme Court observed that “one
49should not, in these matters, lose sight of section 5(3) of the Constitution which lays down the overriding
50principle: a suspect must, whatever the Bail Act says or does not say, be released unless he is brought to
51trial within a reasonable time. (..)”.
52Hence, in the eventuality that no main case is lodged against Applicant by 05.02.2025 without satisfactory
53reasons, Applicant is to be released on bail subject to the fulfilment of the following conditions by him:
54i. to provide a surety in the sum of Rs.20,000/- by bank cheque;
55ii. to enter into a recognisance in the sum of Rs.100,000/-;
56iii. to reside at a fixed place of residence indicated by him to the police;
57iv. to remain at his place of residence daily from 8 pm to 7 am, by virtue of a curfew order hereby
58imposed upon him, and in case of emergency situation, he shall inform the police of same before
59leaving his place of residence during the said curfew hours; and
60v. to report to the police station nearest his place of residence twice daily, 7 days a week, once
61between 7 am and 10 am and once between 4 pm and 7 pm.
62The matter is, therefore, fixed Pro-Forma for 05.02.2025.
63Z Cassamally (Dr)
64District Magistrate

CASE
Primedor Julian Colfir (Applicant) v Police (Respondent), headed *J P Colfir v Police*. 2024 BRC 819. 16 December 2024.

COURT & BENCH
Bail and Remand Court of Mauritius. Dr Z Cassamally, District Magistrate, sitting alone. Ruling delivered by the District Magistrate. No separate or dissenting judgment.

FACTS
The Applicant was provisionally charged in the Bail and Remand Court with sequestration in breach of section 258(1) of the Criminal Code and applied for bail, unrepresented by counsel. The Police objected on the ground that, if released, he was likely to reoffend. The enquiry officer, Sergeant Toolsee, testified that the Applicant had confessed to the offence. A PF 15 and a certificate of the Mauritius Police Force, produced as Doc X and Doc PPU, showed that the Applicant had been sentenced to imprisonment in almost every year between 2014 and 2023 and had incurred the present provisional charge while on bail. The Applicant had been held in custody since 4 February 2024, with no indication when the main case would be lodged. The Court had to decide whether to refuse bail for the risk of reoffending under section 4(1)(a)(ii) of the Bail Act 1999 and, given the length of detention without charge, whether continued detention was consistent with section 5(3) of the Constitution.

HELD
1. On the evidence — the Applicant's confession, spoken to by the enquiry officer, together with the documentary record of near-annual custodial sentences between 2014 and 2023 and of a fresh provisional charge incurred while on bail — the risk that the Applicant would commit a further offence if released was a real and plausible one within section 4(1)(a)(ii) of the Bail Act 1999. (p. 1–p. 2)
2. Applying the balancing rationale of Maloupe, bail fell to be refused because the imposition of conditions was unlikely to reduce the risk of reoffending to a negligible level, having regard to the weight which the presumption of innocence carries in the balance. (p. 2)
3. The Bail Act operates subject to the overriding principle in section 5(3) of the Constitution that a suspect must be released unless brought to trial within a reasonable time; the Applicant having been detained since 4 February 2024 with no indication when the main case would be lodged, he was to be released on conditions if no main case was lodged against him by 5 February 2025 without satisfactory reasons. (p. 2)

Immediate release on bail refused.
Applicant ordered to be released on bail, on conditions of surety, recognisance, fixed residence, a night curfew and twice-daily reporting, in the event that no main case is lodged by 5 February 2025 without satisfactory reasons; matter fixed Pro-Forma for 5 February 2025.

POINTS OF LAW
1. Under section 4(1)(a)(ii) of the Bail Act 1999 a court may refuse to release a defendant or detainee on bail where there is reasonable ground to believe that, if released, he is likely to commit an offence other than one punishable only by a fine; the risk of offending must be a real one supported by adequate reasons, and the danger of a serious offence being committed while on bail must be a plausible one.
2. At the pre-trial stage a defendant should normally be released on bail if the imposition of conditions reduces the risks of absconding, of prejudice to the administration of justice and of harm to society to a negligible level, having regard to the weight which the presumption of innocence carries in the balance; where the imposition of conditions is unlikely to render those risks negligible, bail is to be refused.
3. Whatever the Bail Act provides, bail is governed by the overriding principle in section 5(3) of the Constitution that a suspect must be released unless he is brought to trial within a reasonable time.

CASES CITED
Deelchand v The Director of Public Prosecutions and others [2005 SCJ 215] — applied (adopted the requirement that the risk of offending be a real one supported by adequate reasons): p. 1
Clooth v Belgium [1991] ECHR 71 (12 December 1991) — applied (foreign; ECtHR decision, adopted via Deelchand for the requirement that the danger of a serious offence being committed on bail be a plausible one): p. 1
Maloupe v District Magistrate of Grand Port i.p.o. Director of Public Prosecutions [2000 SCJ 223] — applied (its pre-trial bail balancing rationale governed the refusal of bail): p. 2
Hossen v District Magistrate of Port Louis [1993 MR 9] — applied (its statement of the overriding constitutional principle under section 5(3) required conditional release for delay): p. 2
