12JUDGMENT
13This is an appeal from a judgment of the Black River District Court finding the
14appellant guilty of the offence of driving a motor vehicle on a road with alcohol concentration
15in his blood exceeding the prescribed limit, in breach of section 123F(1)(a)(3) coupled with
16section 52 of the Road Traffic Act.
17The case for the prosecution was that on 18 February 2009 at about 00.15 a.m.,
18two police officers on patrol stopped the appellant who was riding his motorcycle near NHDC
19Bambous for a routine check and noticed he was smelling of alcohol. A breath test at the
20police station revealed positive and subsequently a laboratory test of his blood at the
21hospital confirmed the presence of 77 milligrams of alcohol in his blood.
22The version of the appellant, who was assisted by Counsel and had pleaded not
23guilty to the charge, and who gave sworn evidence, was that on the material date he had
24been pushing his motorcycle due to a flat tyre from Canot to Bambous where he bought
253 bottles of beer and continued pushing his motorcycle when he was stopped by the police
26some 500 metres after. In his statement he added that the two police officers asked him
27whether he had a problem to which he replied that he had a burst tyre. The police officers
28asked him for his driving licence and where were his insurance vignette and the letter “L”.
29The police officers then informed him that the insurance vignette, which was affixed
30underneath his fuel tank, was not visible and insisted upon him dismantling his fuel tank,
31which he refused to do. He was then allowed to proceed on his way. Reaching near his
32house, he was again stopped by the police who informed him that he had to undergo an
33
342
35alcohol test at the police station. In Court, he added that his wife came out and witnessed
36the scene.
37The appellant’s wife deposed and confirmed his version that the police had stopped
38him near their house and that at the material time he was pushing his motorcycle which had
39a flat tyre. She added that after her husband had been taken away by the police, she
40phoned a neighbour to help her to put the motorcycle in her yard.
41Mr Parmensingh, the neighbour, confirmed that on 19 February 2009, he answered a
42phone call of the appellant’s wife at 00.30 a.m., went to the appellant’s place, put the
43motorcycle which had a flat tyre in the appellant’s yard and accompanied the appellant’s wife
44to the police station.
45In his address to the trial Court, learned Counsel for the appellant (then accused)
46submitted that his client did not contest that he was under the influence of alcohol on the
47material night. What he contested was the version of the prosecution that at the material
48time he was riding his motorcycle. It was also learned Counsel’s submission that as no
49police enquiry had been conducted to confirm the appellant’s version that he had a flat tyre
50and was pushing his motorcycle, therefore, latter’s version was to be preferred. He further
51submitted that there was a material discrepancy in the prosecution’s version, namely in the
52evidence of PC Nunkoo, one of the police officers who was on patrol and had stopped the
53appellant, that he had seen the appellant zigzagging on the road when no such evidence
54was to be found in his statement or was forthcoming from PC Beeharry, the other police
55officer on patrol.
56The learned Magistrate, after a thorough analysis of the evidence led by the
57prosecution, the appellant and his witnesses, preferred the prosecution’s version that the
58appellant was riding and not pushing his motorcycle when he was stopped for a routine
59check. She took into consideration the discrepancy in the evidence of PC Nunkoo and
60PC Beeharry on the issue of zigzagging but found it was not of such a nature as to seriously
61and adversely affect the prosecution case.
62On the submission made to her that no police enquiry was conducted into the
63defence of the appellant that his motorcycle had a flat tyre, she found that any such enquiry
64would have served no purpose as this version “was given several months afterwards”.
65She rejected the appellant’s contention that his version of a flat tyre had been given in an
66earlier statement on the 19 February 2009 as she found that neither was she in presence of
67that statement nor was there confirmation from the prosecution witnesses of that statement
68
693
70having been given. She further found untenable the submissions of learned Counsel that
71the police enquiry was tainted with a major flaw.
72The learned Magistrate accordingly found the charge proved against the appellant
73and sentenced him to pay a fine of Rs 10,000. She also disqualified him from driving all
74types of vehicles for a period of 8 months and ordered his licence to be endorsed.
75The appellant now appeals against his conviction on five grounds which seek to
76impugn the factual appreciation of the evidence by the trial Court as well as the conclusions
77reached.
78We have in the light of the submissions of learned Counsel for the appellant reviewed
79the evidence on record as well as the findings of the trial Court but we have found no reason
80to depart therefrom. It was argued by learned Counsel for the appellant that the answer of
81PC Rossan to the question whether the police ought to have verified the tyre of the
82motorcycle was an admission by the police that such verification was an important aspect of
83the enquiry. In the absence of any verification on the part of the police, the learned
84Magistrate ought to have accepted the appellant’s version.
85The question that was put to PC Rossan and his answer which the learned
86Magistrate recorded verbatim read as follows:-
87“Q: Yes the tyres should have been verified
88A: There were other contraventions established against Accused.”
89In our view this answer of the witness is not indicative that he had accepted the
90suggestion of learned Counsel that a verification of the appellant’s motorcycle tyre was an
91important aspect of the police enquiry which the police had omitted to do. We note that
92when reciting the evidence of PC Rossan, the learned Magistrate stated “...He [PC Rossan]
93added that he should have verified whether the tyre was flat…” That part of the learned
94Magistrate’s recital of the evidence of PC Rossan is incorrect. Be that as it may, we take the
95view that this is only a minor lapsus on the part of the learned Magistrate with no adverse
96effect on the soundness of her overall assessment of the evidence. Her finding that the
97absence of an enquiry into the alleged burst tyre was not fatal to the prosecution’s case and
98her reasoning that in any event an enquiry would have served no purpose is unimpeachable.
99This complaint has, therefore, no merit.

1014
102Criticisms were also levelled against the learned Magistrate’s finding that the
103appellant had given his version several months later and that an enquiry into his version
104would have been pointless. We were referred to the testimony of PC Rossan in support of
105the submissions that the latter had confirmed the appellant’s version that he had given a
106statement to the police on the day following the incident wherein he had mentioned his flat
107tyre.
108In the course of the cross-examination of PC Rossan, the officer who had recorded a
109statement from the appellant on 8 October 2009, it was put to him that the appellant’s
110version given on 8 October 2009, namely that he had a flat tyre and was pushing his
111motorcycle when he was stopped by the police, was similar to his version that he had
112allegedly given in a statement on 19 February 2009. The answer of the witness was that
113since he was not in presence of the statement of 19 February 2009, he could not confirm
114whether the appellant had indeed given similar versions on 19 February and 8 October 2009
115respectively.
116In view of the answer of PC Rossan and the fact that the statement of 19 February
117was not produced before the learned Magistrate, her reasoning for rejecting the appellant’s
118contention regarding the tenor of a statement allegedly given on 19 February 2009, shortly
119after he was stopped by the police, is irreproachable. The criticisms levelled against the
120learned Magistrate are accordingly devoid of any merit.
121Learned Counsel for the appellant also found fault with the learned Magistrate’s
122assessment of the appellant’s credibility. His submissions were that the learned Magistrate
123has placed too much emphasis on a discrepancy she had noted in the appellant’s evidence,
124regarding the day on which he had partaken alcoholic drinks, to reject wholesale his
125testimony and to find him an unreliable witness.
126The record shows that the appellant stated in examination-in-chief that he had
127alcoholic drinks on the eve whereas in cross-examination he admitted that he had drunk
128alcohol on that very day. In view of the contradictory answer given by the appellant which is
129borne out by the record, the complaints against the findings of the learned Magistrate that
130the appellant’s version was tainted with an important discrepancy are unfounded.
131The appellant also complained that the learned Magistrate resorted to speculations
132and conjectures not borne out by the evidence before her in rejecting his version that he had
133been pushing and not riding his motorcycle all the way from Canot to Bambous and that on
134his way home bought 3 bottles of beer.
135
1365
137We note from her judgment that the learned Magistrate rejected this version of the
138appellant after having found that his contention that he had bought beer at a time when his
139motorcycle allegedly had a burst tyre was not found in his statement given on 8 October
1402009. We have perused the appellant’s statement and his testimony in Court. It is indeed
141true that his version regarding the buying of beer was said for the first time in Court.
142The learned Magistrate’s remarks which the appellant complained of are in the
143following terms:- “...I find it hard to believe that one would buy beer at such odd hours whilst
144at the same time going through the hassle of having to push an autocycle all the way from
145Canot to Bambous. The main concern of someone who has a breakdown at this time of the
146night would be to get home as soon as possible...”
147Her reasons for doubting the veracity of the appellant’s version as set out above are
148not conjectures and surmises but are in fact a matter of common sense. There is nothing
149sinister in her reasoning which, in the circumstances, we find was fully warranted.
150Lastly, it was argued that on the evidence adduced before her, the learned
151Magistrate was wrong to find that one of the essential elements of the offence of driving
152under the influence of alcohol, which is that the appellant was at the material time riding his
153motorcycle, had been proved beyond reasonable doubt. It was also argued that her reasons
154for rejecting the appellant’s version that he had a flat tyre and that of his witnesses are
155flawed.
156The learned Magistrate had the benefit of seeing and listening to the witnesses
157deposing before her. It has not been established that her assessment and appreciation of
158the evidence that was before her, her appraisal of the credibility of the witnesses for the
159prosecution and the defence and her factual findings were perverse or manifestly ill-founded
160so that we see no cause to intervene. On the contrary, the learned Magistrate’s factual
161findings are plainly borne out by the evidence before her. Having heard the witnesses,
162it was plainly open to her to accept the prosecution’s version and to reject that of the
163appellant and his witnesses. We are, accordingly, unable to find fault with the learned
164Magistrate’s appreciation and analysis of the evidence which was fully warranted.
165Grounds 1, 2, 3 and 5 accordingly fail.
166Ground 4 is to the effect that the learned Magistrate was wrong to hold that the
167discrepancy in PC Nunkoo’s testimony was not so serious. In his submissions, learned
168Counsel for the appellant made a comparison between the testimony of PC Nunkoo and
169PC Beeharry, the two police officers who had stopped the appellant for a routine check.
170
1716
172He then argued that the testimony of PC Nunkoo that he saw the appellant zigzagging on
173the road and the absence of any such evidence from PC Beeharry when testifying was a
174material discrepancy in the prosecution’s case. Learned Counsel went on to submit that the
175learned Magistrate was, accordingly, wrong not to have given sufficient weight to the
176inconsistency in the testimony of PC Nunkoo which heavily impinged on the crux of the case
177which was whether the appellant was riding or pushing his motorcycle.
178In her judgment, the learned Magistrate considered this aspect of the evidence of
179PC Nunkoo and his admission that he had not mentioned same in his statement and had this
180to say: “...As for the version of PC Nunkoo, I have borne in mind a slight discrepancy in his
181version as regards the fact that the autocycle was zigzagging but I find that such
182discrepancy is not so serious as to adversely affect the case for the prosecution and the
183overall reliability and credibility of PC Beeharry who stood as a witness of truth…”
184We agree with the learned Magistrate’s reasoning that save for this contradiction in
185the testimonies of PC Nunkoo and PC Beeharry, their evidence, otherwise, tallied and was
186along the same lines. We find accordingly no merit in the complaint levelled against the
187learned Magistrate of her assessment of the credibility of the two police officers. Ground 4
188equally fails.
189All the grounds of appeal having failed, the appeal is dismissed with costs.

Headnote

Facts

On 18 February 2009, the appellant was stopped by police for a routine check and tested positive for alcohol, with a laboratory blood sample revealing 77 milligrams of alcohol. The appellant claimed he was pushing his motorcycle due to a flat tyre and had purchased three bottles of beer. The trial court rejected his account and convicted him of driving a motor vehicle with excess alcohol concentration under section 123F(1)(a)(3) of the Road Traffic Act, imposing a fine of Rs 10,000, an 8-month driving disqualification, and licence endorsement. The appellant appealed on five grounds, challenging the trial court's factual findings and credibility assessments.

Held

  1. An appellate court will not overturn a trial judge's factual findings and assessments of witness credibility unless they are found to be perverse or manifestly ill-founded.

    L157-L159 — deposing before her. It has not been established that her assessment and appreciation of the evidence that was before her, her appraisal of the credibility of the witnesses for the prosecution and the defence and her factual findings were …
    It has not been established that her assessment and appreciation of the evidence that was before her, her appraisal of the credibility of the witnesses for the prosecution and the defence and her factual findings were perverse or manifestly ill-founded so that we see no cause to intervene.

  2. The trial judge's finding that the absence of police enquiry into a defendant's claim of a flat tyre is not fatal to the prosecution's case, particularly where such enquiry would have served no practical purpose, is sound appellate law.

    L96-L98 — effect on the soundness of her overall assessment of the evidence. Her finding that the absence of an enquiry into the alleged burst tyre was not fatal to the prosecution’s case and her reasoning that in any event an enquiry would have ser…
    Her finding that the absence of an enquiry into the alleged burst tyre was not fatal to the prosecution's case and her reasoning that in any event an enquiry would have served no purpose is unimpeachable.

  3. A minor inconsistency in the testimony of one prosecution witness that is not corroborated by a co-witness does not seriously undermine the reliability of the prosecution's case where their other evidence is consistent and aligned.

    L184-L186 — We agree with the learned Magistrate’s reasoning that save for this contradiction in the testimonies of PC Nunkoo and PC Beeharry, their evidence, otherwise, tallied and was along the same lines. We find accordingly no merit in the complai…
    We agree with the learned Magistrate's reasoning that save for this contradiction in the testimonies of PC Nunkoo and PC Beeharry, their evidence, otherwise, tallied and was along the same lines.

Disposition

All the grounds of appeal having failed, the appeal is dismissed with costs.

Points of law

  1. A credibility assessment may take into account whether a defendant's testimony contains internal contradictions regarding the timing of events.

    L128-L130 — alcohol on that very day. In view of the contradictory answer given by the appellant which is borne out by the record, the complaints against the findings of the learned Magistrate that the appellant’s version was tainted with an important…
    In view of the contradictory answer given by the appellant which is borne out by the record, the complaints against the findings of the learned Magistrate that the appellant's version was tainted with an important discrepancy are unfounded.

  2. Common sense considerations are appropriate and legitimate in assessing the credibility of a witness's account of events.

    L147-L149 — Her reasons for doubting the veracity of the appellant’s version as set out above are not conjectures and surmises but are in fact a matter of common sense. There is nothing sinister in her reasoning which, in the circumstances, we find wa…
    Her reasons for doubting the veracity of the appellant's version as set out above are not conjectures and surmises but are in fact a matter of common sense.

  3. A trial judge may base rejection of a defence claim partly on the fact that the claim was not mentioned in an earlier statement, particularly where that statement is not produced in evidence before the court.

    L116-L120 — In view of the answer of PC Rossan and the fact that the statement of 19 February was not produced before the learned Magistrate, her reasoning for rejecting the appellant’s contention regarding the tenor of a statement allegedly given on …
    In view of the answer of PC Rossan and the fact that the statement of 19 February was not produced before the learned Magistrate, her reasoning for rejecting the appellant's contention regarding the tenor of a statement allegedly given on 19 February 2009, shortly after he was stopped by the police, is irreproachable.
