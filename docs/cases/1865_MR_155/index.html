92At the hearing of the Appeal, the Hon. L. Arnaud and H. Koenig appeared
93for the several Appellants Montille or de Montille in support of their Appeal
94and the Substitute Procureur and Advocate General for the Crown supported the
95Conviction.
96
97The main ground of Appeal rested upon at the trial, though not stated in
98the reasons of appeal, but not objected to by the Crown, was 1o a plea to the
99jurisdiction.
100In support of this reason or plea Arnaud and Koenig referred the Court to
101the text of the District Magistrate's Ordinance (Criminal Side) Art. 101 which
102is thus worded: "Every District Court shall be a Court of Criminal
103Jurisdiction, and shall have cognizance of all questions of fact and law in all
104cases of crimes or of offences not punishable by death or transporting; provided
105that it shall not be lawful for any District Magistrate to award, against any
106offender, a severer punishment than imprisonment for a period not exceeding
107twelve months, with or without hard labor or any not exceeding œ 50 steer. and
108when the Magistrate shall think the case liable to a severer punishment, under
109the Penal Code, he shall proceed in the manner prescribed by Art. 47 of the
110present Ordinance.
111Counsel contended that the District Magistrate had departed from the law
112by a cumulation of punishments which, the text of the law required, should ever
113be kept distinct; that he had not only awarded the maximum of imprisonment but
114also the maximum of the fines, whereas he should have confined himself to the
115award of one or the other punishments, in obedience to the letter of the
116Ordinance which, being a penal statute, required to be construed strictly. That
117a Magistrate acting without the limits of his jurisdiction was as much non judex
118as one who, without a commission, might be bold enough to take upon himself to
119sit in judgment over Her Majesty's subjects. That the Decision given in the one
120as in the other case would be, in law, any thing but a Judgment, a mere nullity,
121which, as such, could not be sustained, and must be set aside.
122The next ground of appeal urged by Arnaud was the Badness of the
123Conviction, which had been based and given upon facts of which the Appellants
124were not accused, and against which they could not offer a defence: such as for
125instance the orders alleged to have been given, by the Montille or de Montille
126for the sequestration charged. - These alleged orders are not mentioned in the
127Criminal Information, nor is the date mentioned when the alleged orders were
128given. Had a time certain or approximate been stated in the Criminal
129Information, the Defendants (now Appellants) would have been placed in a
130position to prove the non-giving of any such orders, and thus exonerated
131themselves from the guilt imputed to them of having ordered the commission of an
132illegal act, of which they were suspected, it is true, but of which they could
133not have been convicted, had no evidence been allowed of the existence of such
134alleged and non charged orders; not only was that evidence inadmissible, in law,
135but also irrelevant to the issue.
136The charge was that the Appellants, Montille or de Montille, "had
137sequestrated" the Indian in the Criminal Information mentioned, which
138necessarily implied that they had, in their own proper persons done so, and not
139by the instrumentality of others under their orders.
140Upon such a Charge, proof was required of the active and personal part
141taken by each of the accused in the sequestration of the Indians. In default of
142any such proof an acquittal, as to them at least, must have been recorded. But
143the allowance, by the Magistrate, of evidence on facts not charged, has led to
144the verdict complained of. If it should be said that the evidence received were
145not inadmissible it was certainly irrelevant, for proof that orders had been
146given, by the appellants, for the sequestration charged was no evidence of the
147Appellants having, with their own hands personally sequestrated the Indians
148mentioned, and could not conduce to the decision of the issue raised (viz:)
149
150Whether or not the Appellants suspected of the sequestration charged had
151personally taken a guilty part in such sequestration.
152In answer to the several grounds of Appeal the Crown Counsel
153unhesitatingly supported the Conviction as sound in fact and in law.
154The fact had been found by the Magistrate not an questionable as alleged,
155but on most satisfactory evidence.
156With the merits or weight of this evidence, the appellate jurisdiction had
157nothing to do; of this the Magistrates alone are Judges (Paley Conviction, P.
158178 § 6) and allowing the evidence to be as unsatisfactory as it was said to be,
159the only point of the consideration of the Court of Appeal was not the
160correctness of the conclusion drawn from the evidence, but merely whether there
161was, in this as in all similar cases, sufficient legal evidence to go to Jury.
162Beyond that the Court will not and cannot exercise judgment upon the credit or
163weight due to the facts from which the conclusion is drawn (Paley on Convictions
164P. 178 § 6).
165The finding of the Magistrate is binding upon the Court of Appeal, and the
166latter has merely to ascertain that the punishment be appropriate to the
167offence. If so, to affirm. If not, in terms of Art. 123 of the District Ord.
168(Criminal Side) to amend such Conviction.
169With reference to the matter of law, the Crown Counsel maintained that the
170power of cumulating the two punishments was strictly legal.
171Art. 101 of the District Criminal Ordinance, in defending the jurisdiction
172of the District Court, say in express terms: "Every District Court shall be a
173Court of Criminal Jurisdiction, and shall have cognizance of all questions of
174fact and law, in all cases of crimes or of offences, not punishable by death or
175transportation." Hence it necessarily follows that the offence charged in this
176case, and tried by the District Court of Grand Port, not being a crime or
177offence punishable by death or transportation, was clearly within the
178jurisdiction and cognizance of the District Court.
179It had been said, in placing within the jurisdiction and cognizance of the
180District Court all crimes or offences not punishable by death or transportation,
181the law had very wisely and carefully specified the nature and limited the
182duration and amount of the punishments to be awarded. "Provided," say's Art.
183101, "that it shall not be lawful for any District Magistrate to award against
184any offender a severer punishment than imprisonment for a period not exceeding
18512 months with or without hard labor or (not) and any fine not exceeding œ 50
186sterlg." That the Magistrate in awarding imprisonment and a fine against the
187Appellants had committed an illegality which vitiates the Conviction, which must
188necessarily be quashed.
189In assuming that the Magistrate had not the power to award imprisonment
190and a fine, and that he should have awarded imprisonment singly, or a fine
191singly, it did not follow that this departure from the text quoted was such an
192illegality as necessarily to draw after it the nullity and quashing of the
193Conviction appealed from. That in so doing he may have exceeded his power of
194punishment might safely be granted. But what would be the consequence of that
195excess of power? Not that the Conviction on that account shall be void and null
196in law and quashable, but amendable by the Appellate Court, in terms of Art. 123
197of Dist. Crim. Ord. by reducing the punishment within the limits of the law, or
198
199by substituting the punishment decreed by law should a wrong punishment have
200been erroneously awarded.
201The Crown Counsel went further and contended that there was, in this case,
202no necessity for the exercise, by the Court, of its power of amendment in as
203much as the cumulation of punishment complained was perfectly legal.
204After giving jurisdiction to the Magistrate over all crimes or offences
205not punishable by death or transportation, the article states, said the Crown
206Counsel, the two kinds of punishments to be awarded by the District Magistrate,
207either imprisonment not exceeding twelve months, or a fine not exceeding œ 50;
208should the Magistrate elect for an imprisonment, he is warned that this
209punishment shall not exceed 12 months, should he elect for a fine, he is told
210that this fine shall not exceed £ 50. But whether he elects one of the
211punishments, or makes up his mind to award both the one or the other, he is
212bound, in either case, to see that the imprisonment awarded do not exceed 12
213months and the fine œ 50. Hence it was inferred that the place of jurisdiction
214was not supported, either by the letter or the spirit of articles quoted, and
215must therefore be overruled and the Conviction affirmed.
216In their reply Arnaud and Koenig again laid great stress upon the wording
217of Art. 101 which, in mentioning the punishment to be awarded by the District
218Magistrate, enacts that these punishments shall be imprisonment or (not and)
219fine and that "it shall not be lawful for any "District Magistrate" to award
220any other punishment but the two above mentioned for the time and amount
221specified by the Article and not otherwise. Were the construction put upon Art.
222101, by Crown Counsel, sound, the result would be that, by cumulating the two
223punishments, a District Magistrate would be empowered to do indirectly, as in
224this case, that which the law has said that it shall be lawful for him to do.
225The Magistrate has given the maximum of imprisonment and the maximum of fin;
226after which he awards that, on default of immediate payment of the fine, the
227defaulters do undergo an additional imprisonment of so many weeks or months,
228thus exceeding, as to one of the accused, by 3 months, maximum of imprisonment
229and in fact awarding an imprisonment of fifteen months.
230This very excess of power establishes the Magistrate's want of
231jurisdiction and entitles the Appellants to claim at the hands of the Court the
232quashing of the Conviction appealed from.
233JUDGMENT
234I shall deal first with the plea of jurisdiction which, if allowed by the
235Court, must necessarily draw after it the quashing of the Conviction, and thus
236render needless any enquiry into the merits of the other objections taken
237against the Conviction.
238[MR p 158] Before the establishment of the District Courts in this Island,
239there was but one Court: "the Tribunal Correctionnel," empowered to take
240cognizance of all the cases, now placed within the jurisdiction of the District
241Court. "Le Tribunal de Première Instance connaitra, sous le titre de Tribunal
242Correctionnel, de tous les délits et contraventions dont la peine excède 5 jours
243d'emprisonnment et dix livres sterling d'amende. (Art. 131 Code d"Instruction
244Criminelle).
245
246The "Tribunal Correctionnel" was empowered, on conviction, to award the
247following punishments: Art. 7 (Penal Code). 1o Imprisonment with or without
248labor.
2492o Interdiction for a term from Civic, Civil or Family rights.
2503o Fine.
251Art. 9 - Fine and special forfeiture are punishments common to Criminal,
252Correctional and Simple Police. (Police matters).
253Art. 31 - "When fines and costs of suit have been decreed in favor of the
254Crown if the offender, after the expiration of his punishment shall not pay the
255amount thereof, he shall suffer imprisonment for as many days as he owes
256shillings; nevertheless such imprisonment shall not exceed six months."
257Under the head of General provisions the Penal Code, Art. 369, further
258says: "In all cases "where a fine is ordained with any punishment, the Court may
259award those punishments together, or one of them only, provided such punishment
260shall, in no case, be less than the punishment of simple police."
261This last article and Art. 9 of the Penal Code shew that the various
262Courts of Criminal jurisdiction, from the highest to the lowest had power,
263either to cumulate the punishment of imprisonment and fine where so ordained by
264law, or to award either the one or the other singly.
265Had the District Ordinance (on the Criminal side) been silent as to the
266punishments, to be awarded by the District Courts, these Courts would have
267inherited the powers of the late "Tribunal Correctionnel" and among the power of
268cumulation complained of.
269Under the Penal Code the "Tribunal Correctionnel" might have given two
270years' imprisonment for the same offence which at the present day cannot be
271visited but with an imprisonment not exceeding twelve months. The fine, in a
272like manner, might have reached a higher amount than œ 50 allowed by the
273District Court, (See C.P. Art. 230 and 258).
274The reduction of the District Court's powers of punishment, when compared
275with the powers of the "Tribunal Correctionnel" which it has replaced, is self-
276evident.
277This first part of Art. 101 is undoubtedly a departure from and, in so
278far, a repeal of the old and more extensive powers of the "Tribunal
279Correctionnel."
280Whether any further reduction in the powers of the District court, by
281depriving them of the power of cumulating the two punishments, was contemplated
282by the legislature, may well be doubted.
283The text of Art. 101 District Magistrate's Ord. (Crim. side) apparently
284supports the opinion expressed by Counsel on this point. "Provided," says the
285Art., "that it shall not be lawful for any District Magistrate to award a
286severer punishment than 12 months imprisonment with or without hard labor or not
287and any fine not exceeding œ 50 sterling.
288But if the word or, used in the Article, is to lead to the conclusion that
289the District Magistrate can award but one of the punishments mentioned, what is
290to become of the provisions of the Penal Code requiring that certain crimes or
291
292offences be visited with the two penalties of imprisonment and labor? - Either
293the requirements of the Penal Code must be disobeyed, or if obeyed the
294Magistrate must necessarily have the power of cumulating the two punishments in
295cases not punishable by death, transportation or reclusion.
296That, in such cases of an enacted cumulation of punishments by the Penal
297Code, the District Magistrate shall not award a severer punishment than twelve
298months instead of twenty four months imprisonment, with or without labor, and a
299fine not exceeding œ 50 sterling in lieu of a higher fine which might have been
300awarded by the "Tribunal Correctionnel," is perfectly intelligible, and would
301be so far as a repeal of the powers vested in the "Tribunal Correctionnel," but
302not such a repeal as would annihilate the enactments of the Penal Code of the
303Island.
304If the term of imprisonment or amount of fine be determine by the Penal
305Code, the Magistrate is bound, by the term of imprisonment and amount of fine so
306determined, however unpowered he might be by the District Ordinance to award
307more of the one or of the other.
308Such offences as are punishable, either by imprisonment alone or by a fine
309only, the duration of the one, and the amount of the other being determined by
310the Penal Code, that punishment and that punishment alone, can and must be
311awarded by the District Magistrate notwithstanding an increase of power of the
312District Court Ordinance.
313But, in every case where the duration of imprisonment, or the amount of
314fine is not expressed in the Penal Code, but left to the discretion of the
315Magistrate, then it is that whether he cumulates imprisonment and fine or awards
316the one or the other, as the case may be, he is strictly to conform to the
317requirements of Art. 101 as to the duration of the imprisonment and the amount
318of the fine.
319This Construction reconciles the apparent conflict between the Penal Code
320and the District Court Ordinance, and does not deprive the Penal law of its
321salutary terrors.
322These several desirable ends, secured by the construction put by the Court
323on the enactment of Art. 101, fully warrant the Court in giving it the
324preference over the construction adopted by the defence, and in overruling the
325plea of jurisdiction.
326As to the Magistrate having exceeded the term of imprisonment allowed, by
327decreeing that, in default of the immediate payment of the fines by the
328Appellants, they should respectively undergo a longer or shorter imprisonment,
329it must be borne in mind that "the Justices (District Magistrates) need not,
330upon the Conviction, adjudge that if the penalty be not forthwith paid, the
331offender shall be committed &a., but may, after affirmation of the Conviction
332upon appeal commit the offender for refusing to pay the penalty, (Paley on
333Convictions. P. 195) and Article 116 District Magistrate Ordinance. (Criminal
334side.)
335The imprisonment ordered in the Conviction, on non payment of the fine,
336may the more easily be treated as surplusage, as there was no necessity for its
337appearing at all in the Conviction. Its appearance therein cannot therefore
338vitiate the Conviction or in any way bear on the question of Jurisdiction.
339
340I therefore overrule the plea of jurisdiction and now proceed to dispose
341of the other grounds of appeal.
342And 1o - That which refers to the badness of the condition because of
343uncertainty.
344The uncertainty complained of lies in the fact that the Criminal
345Information does not charge the now Appellants with having given any orders for
346the sequestrations complained of, nor does it mention any date at which such
347orders had been given. Not charged of having given such orders, the accused
348could not be prepared, with counter evidence, to meet an accusation not
349appearing on the face of the Criminal Information.
350The general rule upon this subject, in Criminal as well as in Civil cases,
351is that nothing shall be given in evidence which does not directly tend to the
352proof of disproof of the matter in issue. To this general rule there are
353however some exceptions? Archibold, Crim. Pleads p. 183. See also Taylor's Ev:
354Vol: I p. 194 - § 193 p. 240 § 245 p. 241 § 246 Edit: of 1848.
355The issue in this case was whether the Appellants were or were not guilty
356of the sequestration charged.
357Though they might not have sequestrated personally and with their own
358hands the Indians mentioned, they might nevertheless be guilty of sequestration
359through the instrumentality of others.
360Evidence that the accused had given orders for the sequestration charged,
361tho' not stated in the Criminal Information, was both admissible and relevant,
362directly tending as it did, to the proof of the matter at issue. The Magistrate
363was therefore, justified in allowing the evidence tendered by the prosecution to
364that effect.
365Had the now Appellants been at all embarrassed in the defence by the
366admission of such evidence, or by the absence of the date or dates at which such
367orders had been given, they should have called on the Prosecutor to supply them
368with the necessary particulars, and move the Court for time to adduce evidence
369to rebut the giving of any such orders at the given date of dates. But such an
370application does not appear to have been made; for on reference to the Record, I
371find the Magistrate stating but two objections which were taken before him.
372(viz:) "La première, que la Plainte n'accuse personne d'une manière directe et
373positive. La seconde se refère à la date, et la défense, soutient qu'au 20 Mai.
374Montille était absent." Both of which objections were overruled.
375The Court cannot, on Appeal, entertain objections not previously submitted
376to the Court below and must therefore overrule this objection made at so late an
377hour.
378With regard to the insufficiency of the evidence, in support of the
379conclusion arrived at by the District Magistrate. "The degree of credit due to
380the evidence on either side is entirely for his (the Magistrate's)
381consideration. For Justices, (or District Magistrates) upon these summary
382proceedings, are placed in the situation of a Jury, and their judgment is
383conclusive as to the facts brought before them."
384"It is sufficient, therefore, to authorize a conviction, that there is
385such evidence before the Magistrate as might, in an action, or an indictment, be
386left to the Jury and the Court of King's Bench, when the Conviction, is brought
387
388before it, will not examine further to see whether the conclusion drawn by the
389Magistrate be, or be not, the inevitable conclusion from the evidence." (Paley
390on Con. Ed. of 1838 P. 59 178.180).
391In this case there was sufficient legal evidence which would have been
392left to the Jury, had the Charge been enquired into by the Court of Assize.
393Had the facts found by the Magistrate been found by a Jury, the presiding
394Judge's duty would have been to order the verdict of the Jury to be recorded and
395to proceed to judgment. Sitting here on Appeal and having ascertained that the
396subject matter of the Charge was within the jurisdiction of the District
397Magistrate, that the evidence adduced before him was both legal and sufficient
398to be left to a jury, that the Magistrate placed in the situation of the jury,
399after weighing the evidence on both sides, had come to the conclusion that the
400parties tried before him were guilty of the offence laid to their charge, his
401finding being conclusive as to the facts brought before him, I am precluded
402"from examining further whether the conclusion drawn by the Magistrate be or be
403not the inevitable conclusion from the evidence.
404Such being the case, this Appeal must be dismissed and it is accordingly
405dismissed with Costs. The Conviction is accordingly affirmed.
406But in supporting this Conviction, I shall amend it by reducing the
407punishment awarded, in as much as it has been ordered by the District Court that
408the imprisonment awarded should be undergone with hard labor.
