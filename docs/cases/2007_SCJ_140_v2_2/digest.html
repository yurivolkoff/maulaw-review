2511.0. LOCUS STANDI
2521. Meaning of locus standi under the general law
253Basically, “locus standi” is the Latin for a “place to stand.” In English legal jargon,
254it means “leg to stand on.” In law, it means the right of a litigant to act or be heard before
255the courts. The right of a citizen to act or be heard before the courts could exist as a
256private right or a public right. Locus standi originated in private law but has become "one
257of the most amorphous concepts in the entire domain of public law" today. That right, if
258violated, gives a party his cause of action. Thus, in private law, it is the civil code or a
259statute which gives him his right on which his cause of action may be grounded.
260Likewise, in criminal law, his right is found in the criminal code or a statute for the
261formulation of his cause of action. In public law, his cause of action may be found in a
262prerogative given by the State to sue the State recognized and enforced by the courts
263which allows him to challenge an incorrect decision or a decision incorrectly taken by the
264State. In constitutional law, a number of rights are enshrined in the Constitution, each of
265which affords him a cause of action. Not all acts and doings of human-kind are
266actionable or justiciable before the courts of law. Any person who initiates an action
267against another has to ground it on a right conferred upon him by the law of the land:
268civil, criminal, administrative or constitutional. It is this right which affords him his place
269or leg to stand on before a court of law.
2702. Meaning of locus standi in constitutional law
271
272In constitutional law, the application of locus standi is a lot more flexible. The
273reason is obvious. While the objective behind a civil and a criminal case is to determine
274the respective rights and remedies between the parties to the case being tried, the
275objective behind a Constitutional Court is to render the constitution of the country into a
276living document by translating the rhetoric of the text into a reality in life for the citizens.
277Inasmuch as the Constitution enshrines the fundamental freedoms of the people, the
278constitutional provisions have to be interpreted sui generis and not as would a legislative
279act of parliament:. As Lord Wilberforce stated in Minister of Home Affairs v Fisher [1980
280AC 319 at p. 319, a Constitution on the Westminster model is to be treated:
281“sui generic, calling for principles pf interpretation of its own, suitable to its
282character ….. without necessary acceptance of all the presumptions that are
283relevant to legislation of private law.”
284That trend has been taken since the 1980’s down to this day: see Darmalingum
285v State 1997 MR Attorney General of the Gambia v Momodou Jobe (1984) 3 WLR 174
286at p. 173; Societe United Docks v Government of Mauritius (1985) LRC Const 801 at
287844; Catanic Components Ltd v. Hill & Smith Ltd (1982) RPC 183 (HL).
288For that reason, the persuasive authority of English law on Mauritian law is more
289pertinent in our public law than elsewhere, locus standi being one dynamic area. In
290recent years the rules about standing have been considerably relaxed. Courts in
291England may not grant leave to apply for judicial review unless it considers that the
292applicant has a sufficient interest in the matter to which the application relates: see
293Halsbury’s Fourth Edition, Vol 1. It has been suggested that the key issue is whether
294the applicant can identify some substantial default or abuse and not whether his
295personal rights or interests are involved. A number of cases have considered the
296standing of pressure groups to bring applications for judicial review. In general the
297courts have decided that such groups do have standing to challenge decisions which
298concern their areas of interest and expertise: see IRC v National Federation of Self-

3213. A two-stage process
322Defendants may challenge the existence of the right of a claimant to act or heard
323before the court. They may do so either at the very initial stage or at a later stage. In the
324words of law, at the procedural stage or the hearing stage; or, in technical language on a
325plea in limine or on the merits. Some cases may be so obvious that a court would find no
326difficulty in saying that the applicant has no locus. Thus, if a lover or a mistress starts an
327action for the dissolution of a marriage between a couple the wife or the husband of
328which, as the case may be, he or she is coveting, the court will straightaway say he or
329she has no such right for such an action. That right to seek a divorce belongs to either
330spouse and no third party. Some cases, however, are not so obvious. Thus, in a case
331where one is alleging an oral contract, the dispute may have to go through the various
332
333stages, right up to the bitter end of merits sometimes, before a court is able to determine
334whether the applicant had a right to base his cause of action upon.
335As Halsbury’s Fourth Edition, Vol 1. puts it, in simple or clear cases the
336question whether the applicant has a sufficient interest is determined at the threshold
337stage as a preliminary issue independent of a full consideration of the merits of the
338complaint. However, the grant of permission will not preclude the court itself
339from considering afresh, nor the opposite party from contending, that the applicant had
340no sufficient interest to bring the application on the merits.
341We have made that law our own: see Kishan Quedou v The State of Mauritius
342[2005 SCJ 70], applying IRC v National Federation of Self-Employed and Small
343Businesses Ltd [supra], IRC v National Federation of Self-Employed and Small
344Business Ltd [supra], IRC v National Federation of Self-Employed and Small
345Businesses Ltd [supra]. We need to recall here that we have adopted this approach
346from the treasure of English decisions obtaining in the field of judicial review, from a
347jurisdiction where there is no written constitution to apply to our jurisdiction where there
348is a written constitution. That does not alter the fact that, insofar as the application of a
349Bill of Rights constitutionally enshrined in both jurisdictions is concerned, consideration
350whether the jurisdiction has a written or an unwritten constitution is simply immaterial.
351Accordingly, the test of standing in our law is the very same formulated by the House of
352Lords: see Betsy v Bank of Mauritius [1992 MR 231] and recently Quedou v The
353State of Mauritius [2005 SCJ 70]:
354“The testing of an applicant’s standing is thus a two-stage
355process. On the application for leave (stage one) the test is designed to
356turn away hopeless or meddlesome applications only. But when the
357matter comes to be argued (stage two), the test is whether the applicant
358can show a strong enough case on the merits, judged in relation to his
359own concern with it.”
360
361When considering the standing of an applicant at the screening stage, the
362question is whether it is a case without hope, and falls short of identifying some
363substantial fault or abuse actionable and remediable as a public law remedy.
3644. The minimum requirement of a citizen in a constitutional action
365When the people of Mauritius pledged their faith and Charter-ed the way public
366institutions as well as other citizens should conduct their affairs under the law, they also
367indicated the route anyone among them should take in protection, and for the purpose,
368of enforcing his constitutional rights. For they knew that rights of citizens enshrined in the
369Constitution mean nothing unless they provided therein a mechanism for the purpose of
370enforcing them effectively. They provided for two mechanisms: sections 17 and 83. They
371did not stop at just providing the mechanisms. They set up an amorphous criteria for the
372courts to apply. They knew that, if worded too restrictively, they would stunt democratic
373development and courts could close their doors too readily pushing the people to the
374streets.
375History has shown that judicial denial of justice is the worst form of denials in a
376democratic society. Executive denials may be remedied: the courts are here for it.
377Political denials may be remedied: elections are here for it. Judicial denials may not be
378so easily remedied. Become endemic, systemic and inhibitive, the hemi-paresis long
379out-lives the judges themselves. A Bill of Rights is not a Bill of Anti-Rights and judicial
380inertia is the most pernicious form of systemic inertia in human society. As high as is the
381responsibility of the judges to determine who has a right of access to the court and who
382has not, equally so high is his responsibility to ensure that the Judge does not become a
383castrator of the Constitution.
384That having been said, when the people provided for use of the constitutional
385court for the determination and enforcement of their rights, they did not provide for its
386abuse. For they knew that if their Constitutional Court is not vigilant, its process was
387prone to be abused in various ways. They knew that there might be abuse by vexatious
388and frivolous applications. They knew that some of their brethren are born with varying
389degrees of constructive and destructive propensities. Some are altruists, some
390
391egocentric. Some engage in serious work, some in reckless adventures. Some help.
392Some hamper. The rest falls anywhere in between. Some honestly and genuinely
393believe in their cause, even if mistakenly. Some do not believe in their cause, but others
394believe in it on their behalf, and sometimes correctly. Some are simply deluded about
395themselves and others. Some may come to court requiring a type of airy-fairy justice that
396may not be obtained on earth but dreamt of only in heaven. Some have a pathological
397sense of self-righteousness some a pathological sense of self-doubt. In the search of a
398meaning to their own lives, some organize themselves usefully, some engage in
399personal or professional crusades, some constitute themselves as pressure groups and
400don evocative names to effect change in society or public institutions. Some are busy-
401bees, satellites of either legitimate, vested, overt or covert interests.
402The Constitutional Court’s high responsibility in such complex competing claims
403lies not only in a judicious but also enlightened approach in discerning and deciding the
404merit or demerit of an action under the criteria set down in the two mechanisms provided
405by the people so that their republican and liberal democracy is neither undermined nor
406abused under the name of democracy itself but duly upheld under that name. It is in this
407that standing in the constitutional context is very different from that in other areas of the
408civil and criminal law. While the Courts recognize the constitutional freedoms and
409liberties inherent to the citizen, they are also under a duty to check, in the exercise of
410their authority under the Constitution, his licences and his abuses.
411In setting down the criteria, the people of the country did not require much of their
412brethren. All they required was that their citizen brother should be so good as to indicate
413the right that he claims has been, is being or is likely to be violated with respect to him;
414or how his interests would stand to be affected by the contravention. The Citizen’s Court
415cannot but apply wisely and judiciously the criteria set down, itself bound by the supreme
416principle of constitutionality.

6615.0. THEORETICAL AND SPECULATIVE NATURE OF THE ACTION
662In his response to the submission on the nature of the action by learned counsel
663for the defendants, Mr Hurnam urged before me that all he is seeking is that, in the event
664of a consultation by the President for the appointment of defendant no. 3 as the next

666on the grounds set out pending the determination of this action. My answer to this is that
667I cannot grant such an order for a number of reasons: first, the very nature of the order
668asked is based on a speculation that the President will as per the Constitution appoint no
6693 as the next Chief Justice and before doing so will consult the Prime Minister. Second,
670the futility of the order I would make is apparent by the fact that the President may
671always say: “Consultation or no consultation, stay order or no stay order, I am the
672Constitutional Authority to make this appointment and the Court is specifically under the
673Constitution precluded from entering into the exercise of my authority in my Executive
674function.” And the President would be right.
675In MacNaughton v Mac Naughton’s TRS [1953 SC 387] cited in Planche v
676The Public Service Commission & Anor [1993 SCJ 128 and referred to in A. R. Mir
677and Ors v Director of Public Prosecutions [2006 SCJ 192], the following was cited:
678“Our courts have consistently acted on the view that it is their function in
679the ordinary run of contentious litigation to decide only live, practical questions,
680
681and that they have no concern with hypothetical, premature or academic
682questions ….. The courts are neither a debating club nor an advisory bureau.”
683As it is the plaintiff’s action is based on mere speculation and theory.
6846.0. STAY ORDER
685Mr Hurnam also argued that all he is seeking is a stay in the interests of
686justice otherwise the whole independence of the judiciary will crumble down. Mr Hurnam
687has given “his truth” in the story he has recited in the 28 paragraphs but he has not
688specified in what way that truth of his will compromise the whole independence of the
689judiciary either partially or altogether to lead to its crumbling down. The Attorney-
690General who should be concerned with such a public right is unwilling to join him in that
691view, is objecting to his standing and submitting for a dismissal.
692As has been stated:
693“A stay or even a dismissal of proceedings may ‘often be required
694by the very essence of justice to be done,’ as per Lord Blackburn in
695Metropolitan Bank v. Pooley (1885) 10 App. Cas. 210 at p. 221), so as
696to prevent parties being harassed and put to expense by frivolous,
697vexatious or hopeless litigation (cited with approval by Lawton L.J. in
698Riches v. Director of Public Prosecutions [1973] 1 W.L.R. 1019, p.
6991027; [1973] 2 All E.R. 935 at p. 942.”
700It may be noted that all the alleged acts and doings of defendants No 3, 5, 6, 7
701and 8, if they are found to be true, belong to the field of private law and I note that Mr
702Hurnam has brought actions against them for private remedies. It is a fundamental
703principle of justice that all persons are presumed to be innocent until proved guilty. Not
704until they are proved guilty may they lose their rights to be considered for any
705impeachment. Alternatively, not until actions in disciplinary proceedings have found them
706guilty of misconduct that they may lose such a right. I do not subscribe to the view that
707the JLSC is unable to take a decision that is why Mr Hurnam is coming to this court for a
708
709remedy. Nor do I subscribe to the view that if the allegations of Mr Hurnam were true,
710the present Chief Justice who, on plaintiff’s own admission, has given in to his requests
711and who is the Chairman of JLSC empowered to trigger proceedings for disciplinary
712actions against legal and judicial officers would have readily obliged in any fitting case.
713Hence Mr Hurnam’s claim that the JLSC is incompetent or incapable in the matter of his
714number of allegations simply cannot hold.
715A similar application was done in the case of Lincoln and Ors v the Governor
716General and Ors, Ex parte Lincolns & Ors (supra).The applicants sought an interim
717injunction to restrain the Prime Minister from introducing a Bill in the Legisltaive
718Assembly. Rault J, as he then was, declined jurisdiction. The reasons he gave may
719equally apply to this case. After commenting that the application was far from requiring
720an exercise of discernment and reason, but a “wild jump in the dark,” he added:
721“In my view, the petitioners are trying to drag the Court into what is not a
722judicial ….. arena. I have no intention of following him there. Our Constitution
723clearly distinguishes between the functions of the Judiciary and those of the
724Legislature. …. If a Court of law sought to prevent, or even delay, the introduction
725of a bill, it would not be exercising a judicial power, but usurping a legislative
726function.”
727In the above well-known proposition of law, legislature may be supplanted for
728executive and the constitutional proposition will stand equally good.
729Where the executive exercise of an authority under the law is concerned, an
730injunction or a stay may only be granted where the ground is “sufficiently firmly based to
731justify so exceptional a course:””see R. v Secretary of State for Transport Ex p.
732Factortame Ltd (No. 2) [1991] 1 A.C. 603. That case involved the exercise of
733executive function under a legislation. I am unable to see in his application that the
734ground is “sufficiently firmly based to justify so exceptional a course” as to grant an
735injunction or a stay.
7367.0. PUBLIC RIGHT V PRIVATE RIGHT
737
738From a reading of his plaint, Mr Hurnam’s application seems to be much more
739his personal embarrassment at his having to live and practice under a Chief Justice
740against whom he has so many grievances. True it is that he has coloured the device as
741“interest of justice” and “independence of the judiciary.” However, if that were really the
742case, the Attorney-General – the authority under the law to bring an action in the case of
743a public wrong - would not have raised an objection in law as he has done. He would
744have indeed seen that “the public is sufficiently interested in the dispute to warrant the
745Attorney-General intervening for the purpose of asserting public rights”: see Attorney-
746General v Sharp [1930] All ER 741; Attorney-General v Premier Line [1932] 1 Ch.
747303; Municipal Council of Port Louis v Ibrahim Y.A. [2004 MR 297].
748Finally, I may remark that not every personal grievance becomes a constitutional
749grievance. Not every complaint is actionable. Not every case is justiciable. Mr Hurnam’s
750right would have been affected if he were to show, for example: that as a litigant, he was
751unable to bring an action against the parties against whom he has already brought an
752action; that as a barrister, he was being denied the practice of his profession: and that,
753as a citizen, the new incumbent to the office of the Chief justice was to deny him his
754rights: see Tengur v Ministry of Education [2002 SCJ 48] and Gouriet v Attorney-
755General, Sabapathee v The State [1997 SCJ 337].
756What Mr Hurnam seems to say is that there is a malign crusade against him by
757the Ag Chief Justice. That is not apparent from the facts he has recited in his affidavit of
75818 pages and 28 paragraphs. That at best is his personal and subjective view. The
759defendants concerned have been exercising their authority under the Constitution. I
760cannot extract from his affidavit objective facts which lead me to the inescapable
761conclusion that the Ag. Chief Justice has a personal grudge against the applicant and
762his present plight is the result of the Ag. Chief Justice’s victimization of him. If I were to
763infer that, I would equally have to infer that, on the basis of plaintiff’s own affidavit, that
764the plaintiff is all in crusade against defendants 3, 5, 6 7 and 8, whose only sins have
765been to exercise their power under the Constitution and under the law, as the case may
766be, to take decisions adverse to the plaintiff. Alternatively, I would have had to infer that
767the plaintiff has been engaged in a process of self-victimization for all his other abilities.
768
769If the plaintiff has felt aggrieved, his remedy lies in a personal actions which in the
770circumstances he has brought against them. The mere fact that Mr Hurnam has been
771able to say so much so freely is evidence that justice in Mauritius was never regarded as
772a cloistered virtue: See Ambard v Attorney-General for Trinidad and Tobago [1936]
773AC 322, at p. 335 (PC).
7748.0. CONCLUSION
775In my view, apart from the above legal flaws set out, plaintiff’s action suffers from
776factual flaws. His affidavit is a mixed bag of facts, subjective inferences from facts,
777disputable conclusions, personal views, inadmissible hearsay, citations and events taken
778out of context. I cannot say whether it was to the credit or discredit of the defendants not
779to have objected to such a manner of pleadings which should have contained only
780matters of fact that are necessary to sustain the plaint: see New Supreme Court Rules
7812002, rule 13.
782Be that as it may, from the very averments of the plaintiff, it is clear that he has
783already brought actions against defendants for private remedies against them. If that is
784so, then his qualms would be dealt with before the competent court and his remedies
785would be available there.
786As regards the apparent abuse which plaintiff alleges defendant no 3 has done
787through his niece in the exercise of his office or the alleged use the niece has made of
788her office, it is worth recalling that they smack of an exercise of duty within the ordinary
789course of office. For example, reduction of charges whether in a drugs or other matters
790is properly a discretion of the Director of Public Prosecutions under the Constitution.
791Also, reviews of decisions for prosecutions by the office of the DPP is a matter of almost
792daily occurrence in criminal prosecutions for every citizen. I like to think that every right-
793thinking person and positive thinker in our plural society has long stopped giving every
794decision taken by a public body and adverse to him an impermissible ethnic, racial or
795communal colour. Indeed, when the various allegations with the loaded words and
796expressions are properly examined, they are seen to be matters which not only
797defendant No 3 but also defendant No 5, 6, 7 and 8, as office holders were taking in the
798
799exercise of their public responsibilities. For some of the decisions which were not
800favourable or controversial to the petitioner or his clients, the remedy lay and still lie
801elsewhere, such as in judicial review, appeal or private actions.
802As regards the failings of the JLSC, it is worth noting that it is the Chief Justice
803who chairs the Commission and if the Chief Justice whose ears he seems to have had
804all through did not think it fit to take any action, in what way the sins of others should be
805visited upon defendants no.3, plaintiff does not explain.
806In my consideration, therefore, the petitioner has not succeeded in meeting the
807threshold test of locus standi to show that the events he has enumerated will affect him
808in his rights either as a litigant, as a barrister or as a citizen. He has also not shown in
809what way his qualms are of such a nature that I should order a stay of the constitutional
810process pending the determination of this case. I am unable to see in what way if the Ag.
811Chief Justice is appointed in a substantive capacity by the President who is the sole
812constitutional authority to make the appointment the events, the plaintiff’s activities will
813suffer a set-back, when I take into account the fact that he has a history of litigation, in
814his capacity as a litigant, as a barrister and as a citizen, dating his early professional
815days. From his own story, nothing has stopped him and nothing is likely to, if one goes
816by his story, whether or not defendant no. 3 is appointed.
817The objections of the defendants have been well taken. I dismiss the plaint. With
818costs.
