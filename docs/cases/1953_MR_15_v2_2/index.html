The Queen v. L. G. L'Etendry alias Letendrie
1953 MR 15
The reproduction supplies no date of judgment; the case is reported at 1953 MR 15 (court Record No. 1746). The full name of the accused appears in the judgment as Lux Gabriel Létendry, alias Létendrie.

COURT & BENCH
Supreme Court of Mauritius, sitting as a bench of three judges on two points of law reserved by the trial judge after verdict, under section 168 of the Criminal Procedure Ordinance. The judgment is a single judgment of the court, delivered throughout in the first person plural; there is no separate or dissenting judgment. Of the three judges who sat, the reproduction names only Sir Francis Herchenroder, C.J.; the names of the other two judges are not preserved in the reproduction — a material gap, the court being constituted of three. Simmons, J. was the trial judge, who convicted and sentenced the accused at the First Assizes, 1953, and who stated the case.

FACTS
Lux Gabriel Létendry, alias Létendrie, was tried for murder at the First Assizes, 1953, convicted, and sentenced to death by Simmons, J. After the verdict, defence counsel applied to the trial judge to reserve, for the consideration of three judges, two points of law arising out of the summing-up; the trial judge acceded and stated a case under section 168 of the Criminal Procedure Ordinance.

The two points concerned the direction to the jury on drunkenness as a defence to murder: (1) the effect of, and the distinction between, partial drunkenness (ivresse partielle) and complete drunkenness (ivresse complète); and (2) the trial judge's expressed opinion that a heavy drinker is less influenced by a given quantity of alcohol than a casual drinker. At trial the defence had relied on drink as bearing on intent to kill and premeditation; the defence of complete drunkenness did not arise and was not raised. The issue for the court was whether the summing-up misdirected the jury on the law of drunkenness. As sections 42 and 43 of the Mauritian Penal Code are borrowed from articles 64 and 65 of the French Penal Code, the court examined the position under French law at length before determining the law of Mauritius.

HELD
1. Where a provision of the Mauritian Penal Code is borrowed from, and identical to, a provision of the French Penal Code, it is to be construed by resort to French doctrine and case law. Sections 42 and 43 of the Penal Code Ordinance being borrowed from and identical to articles 64 and 65 of the French Penal Code (save for the words "and in consequence, he shall be acquitted" added at the end of section 42), the Mauritian law of drunkenness as a defence to murder falls to be applied in accordance with French principles, save where those principles rest on provisions having no counterpart in Mauritian law (in the passage stating "The law of Mauritius on the subject").

2. Drunkenness which is involuntary or accidental, does not amount to insanity (démence), and is so complete that the accused was incapable of forming the intent to commit the assault upon the person of his victim is material for the jury; if they find drunkenness of that completeness, all criminal intent is taken away, the act is not criminal, and they should acquit.

3. Partial drunkenness is, as a matter of law, a circumstance which the jury cannot take into account to negative intent to kill or premeditation, or either of them, and cannot use to reduce a charge of murder to a lesser offence. Because in French law partial drunkenness pleaded to a charge of murder can operate only through a declaration of extenuating circumstances under article 463 of the French Penal Code, and Mauritian law contains no equivalent provision, partial drunkenness is not a matter that can be left to the jury in Mauritius.

4. Mauritian law containing no provision equivalent to article 463 of the French Penal Code, the jury is not required to find or declare extenuating circumstances, and the extenuating effect, if any, of partial drunkenness is likewise not a matter for the jury.

5. The trial judge's directions were accordingly correct: he was right to tell the jury that it was not sufficient for the defence merely to establish that the accused had been drinking (passages (a)–(b)); right to treat complete drunkenness as a complete answer to the charge entitling the accused to acquittal (passage (c)); and right to withdraw from the jury both partial drunkenness in relation to intent and premeditation and its possible extenuating effect (passage (d)) (in the passage examining directions (a)–(d)).

6. Withdrawing partial drunkenness from the jury imported no presumption against the accused: intent to kill and premeditation remained to be proved by the prosecution, and the direction did no more than give effect to the rule that, where those matters may reasonably be inferred from the evidence, evidence of partial intoxication cannot be used to rebut the inference.

7. On the second reserved point, the trial judge's expression of opinion that a heavy drinker is less influenced than a casual drinker was at most a misdirection as to the evidence, and doubtfully a "question of law" within section 168 of the Criminal Procedure Ordinance; but even assuming it to be such, a misdirection as to the evidence avails a prisoner only if it is reasonably probable that, but for it, the jury would not have returned the verdict they did, the burden lying on the prisoner. Complete drunkenness not being in issue, the remark could not have affected the verdict, and that burden was not discharged.

Both points of law reserved determined against the prisoner; verdict and sentence of death affirmed.

POINTS OF LAW
1. A provision of the local Penal Code borrowed from, and identical to, a provision of the French Penal Code is to be construed by resort to French doctrine and jurisprudence, save so far as the French principle depends upon a statutory provision that has no counterpart in local law.
2. The effect of drunkenness as a defence to a criminal charge is, under the system followed in Mauritius as in France, a question of substantive law.
3. Complete drunkenness — involuntary or accidental, not amounting to insanity, and so complete that the offender's reason is entirely dethroned and he cannot form any criminal intent — negatives criminal intent and entitles the accused to acquittal. "Complete drunkenness" in this sense does not mean the insensibility of a person so drunk as to be physically incapable of committing the offence at all.
4. Partial drunkenness cannot, as a matter of law, be taken into account by the jury to negative intent to kill or premeditation, and cannot reduce murder to a lesser offence.
5. Where the local law contains no provision equivalent to article 463 of the French Penal Code (extenuating circumstances), the jury is neither required nor entitled to find or declare extenuating circumstances, and the extenuating effect of partial drunkenness is not a matter for the jury.
6. A direction that the jury may not, as a matter of law, consider partial intoxication in relation to intent or premeditation creates no presumption against the accused; the prosecution's burden of proving intent and premeditation from the evidence is unaffected.
7. A misdirection as to the evidence, as distinct from a misdirection in law, will avail a convicted prisoner only where it is reasonably probable that, but for the misdirection, the jury would not have returned the verdict they did; the burden of establishing that lies on the prisoner.
8. (semble) A trial judge's bare expression of personal opinion upon the evidence, unsupported by evidence, is a misdirection as to the evidence and not a "question of law" within section 168 of the Criminal Procedure Ordinance.
9. (obiter) In English law, under R. v. Beard, evidence of drunkenness is to be considered, with the other facts, upon the question of specific intent only where it is such as to render the accused incapable of forming that intent; drunkenness falling short of such incapacity, and showing merely that the mind was so affected that the accused more readily gave way to passion, does not rebut the presumption that a man intends the natural consequences of his acts; and drunkenness not amounting to insanity, when accepted as a defence to murder, reduces the offence to manslaughter.
