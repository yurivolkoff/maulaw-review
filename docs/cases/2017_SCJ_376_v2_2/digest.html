14JUDGMENT
15In an amended plaint with summons (“plaint”) dated 31 October 2011, the plaintiffs
16are suing the defendants for abuse of their proprietary rights, for breach of agreement
17between the plaintiffs and Mr. Bucktawar (the former owner of defendants’ property) and for
18breach of agreement between the plaintiffs and the defendants. The plaintiffs are only
19pressing for the following alternative prayers which read as follows:
20(a) to declare that the defendants have committed an “abus de leur droit de
21propriété” by attempting to build a wall in an attempt to settle scores with the
22plaintiffs especially in view of the tacit agreement which existed between them to
23allow the plaintiffs to use the common road and the concessions made by the
24plaintiffs as regards the constructions by the defendants of a staircase on the
25boundary line separating their properties; and
26(b) to order the defendants to refrain from doing any act which will interfere with the
27plaintiffs’ peaceful and quiet enjoyment of the common road as it exists.
28The plaintiffs aver in the plaint that they acquired a plot of land, in the middle of a
29morcellement at Curepipe, located more specifically at the rear of the defendants’ property.
30The Co-defendants and the plaintiffs have access to the main road, Emile Pitot Street,
31through a common road. It is also averred that in certain title deeds, the common road
32appeared as 1m62cm but in reality it is of an extent of 2m20cm. The plaintiffs allowed the
33defendants to construct a staircase on their boundary line, separating their two properties

144Analysis of facts and findings
145The question which I am called upon to determine is:-
146(a) whether the plaintiffs have proved on a balance of probabilities that there was an
147agreement between them and the previous owner Mr. Bucktawar that the
148plaintiffs were allowed to use the common road in excess of what was mentioned
149in their respective title deed?
150(b) whether the defendants are exercising their proprietary rights in a way “de nuire à
151autrui ou de manière à causer un préjudice hors de proportion” to the plaintiffs.
152The first plaintiff is a retired customs officer and he bought the plot of land from his
153father in 1990 being fully aware that the common road leading to his property is only
1541m62cm. He was acquainted to the previous owner Mr. Bucktawar but he never considered
155requesting the latter to give him a written permission to use the common road in excess of
1561m62cm. His contention is that he could not get a written agreement because of the
157confidence and trust which existed between them.
158Under article 1348 of the Civil Code, oral evidence is admissible, when it has not
159been possible for one of the parties to obtain written proof of the obligation contracted
160towards him. What constitutes impossibility is not defined by law, and the Court is allowed
161complete freedom in deciding in each case, having regard to all the circumstances including
162the intimate, friendly and close relation between the parties, whether or not it was possible
163for the party alleging a certain transaction to obtain written proof thereof. Thus, a son, who
164had received sums of money representing payments of rent due to his mother, and who had
165duly remitted such sums to the latter, was held to be in the moral impossibility from his
166mother written proof of such remittance, (See Dalloz, Répertoire Pratique, V0. Preuve, n.
1671226; D.P.1921.2.40).
168In the present case, the plaintiffs have failed to establish that they had an intimate
169and close relationship with Mr Bucktawar except that the latter bought his property from the
170first plaintiff’s grandfather and that Mr Bucktawar was driving a taxi on the common road.
171Given the nature of the transaction, the relationship between the plaintiffs and Mr Bucktawar,
172the plaintiffs have not proved that due to their intimate relationship, they were in an
173“impossibilité matérielle ou morale” to secure an agreement. It is therefore reasonable to
174conclude that it was possible for the plaintiffs to obtain written proof of the agreement to use
175the excess of the common road, but they did procure one. As per the evidence on record,
176the plaintiffs had the possibility to procure a written agreement from Mr Bucktawar.
177
1786
179The contention of the plaintiffs is that the Court should infer that since they gave
180permission to the defendants to build a staircase on their boundary line and they equally
181gave their consent to the defendants to sell liquor in their shop, there was a mutual
182understanding that the latters would allow them to continue using the common road in
183excess of 1m62cm to get access to their property by car. It is not disputed by the first
184defendant that the plaintiffs had given their authorisation whenever the defendants have
185requested same. However, the defendants contend that the plaintiffs have never asked for
186permission to use part of the defendants’ property. From the evidence on record, it appears
187that the first plaintiff took it for granted that the defendants would always allow him to use the
188common road in excess of 1m62cm. The plaintiffs had the opportunities to negotiate with
189the defendants on not less than two occasions, firstly when he authorised them to construct
190a staircase and secondly when the latter needed their permission to sell liquor in their shop.
191Based on the evidence on record, the plaintiffs have failed to establish that there was an
192agreement between them and the defendants to allow them to use the common road in
193excess of 1m62cm.
194It is appropriate to refer to the report of “constat” of Mr. Coomar Ragoo, Land
195Surveyor, which reads as follows:
196“I, the undersigned Land Surveyor, Coomar Ragoo, certify having repaired
197to place called Curepipe, Emile Pitot Street, in the District of Plaines
198Wilhems at the request of Mr. Bhagwatparsad Caullychurn and others to
199make a “constat” regarding the access for three plots of land shown 1, 2 and
2003 on plan.
201These three plots belonging to Mr. Bhagwatparsad Caullychurn,
202Mr. Cooldeve Caullychurn and Mrs. Parewatee Caullychurn (spouse of
203Mr. Bissoonduth Sambal) following title deeds transcribed in Volumes
204T.V.2049 No.110, T.V.1990 No.29 and T.V. 1990 No.29 respectively have
205no access from any other side of their boundaries. Their only access is the
206one shown on the plan and it leads to the road known as “Emile Pitot
207Street”. And without this access these plots would all have been “enclaved”
208plots.
209The said access as per description in title deeds known as a “common road”
210is of 1m62cm wide but according to my observations on site it has been
211used for a very long time to a larger width of 1m62cm which I found by
212measurement to be 2m40cm wide”.
213

215It is noted that the above “constat” does not make mention that the defendants’ title
216deed refers to the “common road” as being 1m52cm. The report also falls short of stating
217whether the land in excess of 1m62cm belongs to the defendants or whether it is a surplus
218land. The report further does not make mention of the width of defendants’ property though
219Mr. Ragoo testified in Court that the house of the defendants does not encroach on the
220common road. In the absence of a proper survey of the defendants’ property, the Court is in
221the dark as to whether the common road, which is allegedly 2m40cm, existed prior to
222Mr. Bucktawar purchased the property and whether it belonged to the initial owner.
223Mr Ragoo did not conduct a proper history of the land in order to determine the exact layout
224of the common road. In the light of the evidence on record, I am not satisfied that Mr
225Ragoo’s report can be relied upon.
226Having considered the pleadings, the evidence on record and the submissions of
227learned Counsel, I have no hesitation in finding that the plaintiffs have failed to establish on
228a balance of probabilities that there was a tacit agreement from Mr. Bucktawar and the
229defendants to allow them to use the common road in excess of 1m62cm. I am also not
230satisfied that the plaintiffs “n’a pas eu la possibilité matérielle ou morale de se procurer une
231preuve littérale de l’acte juridique.”
232Are the defendants making an abuse of their proprietary right by constructing a wall to
233enclose their property?
234Article 647 of the Civil Code provides that:
235“Tout propriétaire peut clore son héritage, sauf l’exception portée en l’article
236682.”
237It is also apposite to refer to Article 544 of the Civil Code which provides that:
238“La propriété est le droit de jouir et disposer des choses de la manière la plus
239absolue, pourvu qu’on n’en fasse pas un usage prohibé par les lois ou par les
240règlements”.
241The defendants were entitled by law to enclose their property and they have an absolute
242right of the enjoyment of their property.
243I have considered both testimonies of the first plaintiff and the first defendant and I
244note that the first defendant has testified in a straightforward manner. I have no difficulty in
245accepting his evidence as being true. I am not satisfied that the defendants are exercising
246their proprietary rights in an abusive manner and in bad faith.

249The plaintiffs have therefore failed to establish that there was a tacit agreement
250between them and the defendants that they gave their consent for the defendants to build a
251staircase and to sell liquor on the understanding that the latters would allow them to use the
252common road in excess of what is provided in their respective title deed.
253Article 691 of the Civil Code provides that:
254“Les servitudes continues non apparentes, et les servitudes discontinues
255apparentes ou non apparentes, ne peuvent s’établir que par titres.
256La possession même immémoriale ne suffit pas pour les établir, sans cependant
257qu’on puisse attaquer aujourd’hui les servitudes de cette nature déjà acquises
258par la possession dans le pays où elles pouvaient s’acquérir de cette manière”.
259Indeed, as borne out from the evidence on record, the first plaintiff was fully aware
260that the second defendant’s title deed made mention of her property being bordered by a
261common road measuring 1m52cm, yet he made no effort to purchase a right of way in
262excess of 1m52cm to allow him to drive his car thereon.
263The plaintiffs also contend that the measurement of the common road as per their
264title deed is 1m62cm but in reality it is 2m40cm. If the contention of the plaintiffs was
265correct, then the defendants could build their wall without causing any hindrance to the first
266plaintiff to use his car along the common road.
267It is not disputed that the plaintiffs’ land is not “enclavée” as they have a “droit de
268passage” of 1m62cm but the said right of way may be “non-commode” for the first plaintiff to
269drive his car. The plaintiffs can only acquire an excess of 1m62cm of the right of way by
270means of a title deed but they have not done so.
271In the absence of a proper surveyor’s report indicating the width of the defendants’
272property in relation to the common road and a proper history of the layout of the common
273road and for the reasons given above, I am not satisfied that the plaintiffs have proved their
274case on a balance of probabilities. In view of the circumstances of the present case, I non-
275suit the plaintiffs and make no order as to costs.

Headnote

Facts

The plaintiffs purchased land with sole access via a common road measuring 1m62cm according to their title deed. The defendants purchased property with access via the same common road, which their title deed described as 1m52cm. The plaintiffs authorized defendants to construct a staircase on their shared boundary line and granted permission to operate a liquor shop. In December 2010, the defendants marked their boundary line with red paint in preparation to build a wall. The plaintiffs sought an interlocutory injunction; the first defendant undertook not to proceed pending trial.

  1. Under Article 1348 of the Civil Code, oral evidence is admissible to prove an obligation only where a party could not obtain written proof due to material or moral impossibility; mere trust and friendly relations do not constitute such impossibility where the party had adequate opportunity to procure written agreement.
