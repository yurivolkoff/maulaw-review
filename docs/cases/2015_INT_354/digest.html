9JUDGMENT
10The accused is charged with the offence of ‘Smuggling’ in breach of section 156(1)(a) and (2) of the
11Customs Act (“the Act”).
12He pleaded not guilty to the charge and was assisted by Mr G. Glover, S.C.
13Mr S. Bhoyroo, State Counsel, and Mr A. Ramdahen, State Counsel, appeared for the prosecution (at
14different sittings).
15The case for the prosecution
16The evidence for the prosecution is in a gist that during an operation set up and supervised by SP
17Veeramalay (W7), and SP Jugdeo, and involving PS Mooniaruth (W5) and other police officers, on 29
18November 2005, one Nissar Jandoo, coming from Dubai, was profiled as a gold smuggler, and was seen
19to enter a toilet, followed by the accused. Both emerged separately minutes later, the accused keeping his
20left hand in his pocket all the time, until he left the building, when W7 lost sight of him.
21PS Mooniaruth stopped the accused in the car park and disclosed his identity to him and informed him of
22the reason for a search: after a slight hesitation the accused removed his hand from his pocket and gave
23him a brown packet and said “Jandoo fine donne moi”. The parcel was opened in the accused’s presence
24and found to contain yellow jewellery. The accused was questioned and cautioned. The exhibits (28
25pendants, 37 bracelets, 65 pairs of earrings, 5 rings, 10 necklaces, 18 necklaces) were counted at the
26ADSU, Line Barracks and found to weigh 669.8g. They were sealed in five plastic bags and envelopes
27coded FMI/2005, in the presence of the accused: the accused signed on the envelopes. As per procedure,
28they sent the exhibits to the ‘Assay Office’, which is the only Government institution that can certify if
29jewellery is made of gold or copper, etc.
30Following the receipt of a letter dated 7 December 2005 from the police concerning jewellery, Mr T.
31Goorohoo (W17) calculated the duty paid value and put up a report dated 12 January 2006. He took into
32account the appraised value and calculated the VAT applicable at that time (i.e. 15% of the appraised
33value). The Customs Department was the competent authority to deal with the appraised value of
34jewellery, and in this case the appraised value was obtained from the ‘Assay Office’, and the Customs
35Dept. was never involved.

38Mr S. Lutchanah (W15) said that the case of Police v Jandoo Nissar Ahmad, Cause No. 371/2010 was

41The case for the defence
42The accused exercised his right to silence and did not answer any of the questions put to him during the
43recording of his statement dated 30 November 2005 at 3.20pm, as read and produced by Insp. Sooben
44(W1) in Court (Doc. B).
45Discussion
46The accused is being prosecuted under section 156(1)(a) and (2) of the Customs Act1. It is averred in the
47Information that on or about 29 November 2005 he “did wilfully and unlawfully smuggle goods into
48Mauritius, to wit:- (i) twenty-eight (28) pendants; (ii) thirty-seven (37) bracelets; (iii) sixty-five (65) pairs
49of earings; (iv) five (5) rings; (v) ten (10) necklaces/pendants; and (vi) eighteen (18) necklaces. Duty paid
50value = Rs363,093/-.” (sic)
51There is no dispute that the accused was a Customs Officer at the material time, as per the letter dated 5
52December 2005 produced by Mrs S. Auleear (W14) (Doc. A).
53Learned defence counsel has submitted that the information is defective inasmuch as (i) it does not
54contain any averment as to the specific alleged material act and (ii) it does not aver with precision which
55Duty, Excise Duty and Tax the accused intended to evade and it would therefore be unsafe to infer
56fraudulent intent. It was further submitted that the case of Ramoly v The State [1987 MR 29] should be
57distinguished since neither the actus reus nor the mens rea of the alleged offence has been averred and
58this has prejudiced the accused in his defence.
59The prosecution did not offer any submissions.
60“Smuggling” is defined at section 2 of the Act as:
61“any import, introduction, export or attempted import, introduction or export of goods with the
62intent –
63(a) to defraud or evade the payment of duty, excise duty, taxes and other payable charges, as
64the case may be; or
65(b) to evade any prohibition of, restriction on, or regulation as to, the import, introduction or
66export of any goods;”
671 (1) Any person who -
68(a) smuggles into Mauritius any goods;
69(b) …
70(c) …
71shall commit an offence.
72(2) Any person who commits an offence under subsection (1) shall on conviction, be liable
73(a) to a fine which shall –
74(i) where the value of the goods does not exceed Rs1,500, be Rs4,000;
75(ii) where the value of the goods exceeds Rs1,500, be three times the value of the goods or Rs20,000, whichever is the higher,
76(b) to imprisonment for a term not exceeding 8 years, and the goods, the subject-matter of the offence, shall be liable to forfeiture.

79In Ramoly (supra) consignments of goods destined for re-exportation were found in the appellant’s shop
80and as a consequence he was prosecuted before the Intermediate Court for smuggling, under section
81167(i)(a) and (2)(b) of the Customs Act. The Supreme Court found the information to be improperly
82drafted and said:
83“A charge of smuggling must, in its particulars, comprise 2 things: an averment of importation,
84introduction or exportation (or an attempt of one of them) plus an allegation that the accused did
85what he did with intent to defraud the revenue or to evade a prohibition, restriction or regulation.
86No objection to the information was taken at the trial and the record makes it clear that everyone
87understood the charges to mean that the appellant had imported goods with intent to evade a
88restriction on the importation thereof (i.e. one which prevented home entry).”
89It is to be noted that the Supreme Court found that the defect in the information did not vitiate the
90conviction since there was no objection to the information at the trial. In reply to defence counsel’s
91submissions that no objection could be taken in the wake of the decision in the case of Ramoly v R [1985
92MR 611] (where the Court held that the omission of the value of the goods in the information did not
93render it defective and gave an opinion on the word ‘import’, obiter) the Supreme Court said that this
94would not have prevented defence counsel from raising the point that the information did not contain an
95essential element of the offence.
96In I. Jangeerkhan v The State [1997 SCJ 133] although the Supreme Court noted that the particulars
97referred to in Ramoly (1987) were not averred in the information, “from the evidence it appears clearly
98that everyone was aware that the only moot question regarding the intent of the appellant was whether he
99was set to defraud the revenue.” However, the Court went on to say that the “apparent difficulty resides
100with the other missing particulars which normally should be given viz. an averment of import,
101introduction or export.”
102In Rughooputh v State [1997 SCJ 329] the Supreme Court differentiated between the offences of
103smuggling and unlawful possession of goods for which duty had not been paid, as follows:
104“Unlike a charge of smuggling under section 156 (1)(a) of the Act, the intention to defraud or to
105evade payment of duty, levy and taxes is not an element of the offence under section 156(1)(c)(i)
106of the Act...”
107In Director of Public Prosecutions v Iframac Limited [1999 SCJ 9] the respondent was charged with
108having produced a false document to the Customs in breach of section 158(3) of the Customs Act 1988.
109The Supreme Court made a comparison between the offence under section 158(3) and smuggling, saying:
110“intention to defraud is not an element and is not required to be averred in the information or
111established in evidence by the prosecution. Had such an ulterior intent been required, the legislator
112would have provided expressly to that effect, as he has done in relation to the offence of
113smuggling where the prosecution has to aver in the information and establish in evidence the
114intent to defraud or evade the payment only: vide section 2 of the Act.”
115The abovementioned cases make it clear that an averment of importation, introduction or exportation and
116intention to defraud revenue or evasion of prohibition, restriction or regulation are elements of the offence
117of smuggling, so that an information charging an accused under section 156(1)(a) must contain the
118averment “of importation, introduction or exportation (or an attempt of one of them) plus an allegation
119that the accused did what he did with intent to defraud the revenue or to evade a prohibition, restriction or
120regulation.” However, in the mentioned cases the Supreme Court did not conclude that the information,
1213
122
123which lacked the said averments, were defective, this because no objection was taken to the information
124at trial stage and it was understood that the charges meant the goods were imported with intent to evade a
125restriction on the importation thereof.
126In the present case, there is neither the averment of the specific material act committed by the accused nor
127the averment that the accused had the intent to defraud the revenue or to evade payment of duty. The
128cases of Ramoly 1987 and Jangeerkhan can be distinguished from the present matter inasmuch as here
129the defence has submitted that the information is defective and the evidence on record does not disclose
130that everyone understood the charge to mean that the accused had imported goods with intent to evade a
131restriction or to defraud the revenue. I therefore find, in the light of the pronouncements of the Supreme
132Court, that the information is defective as it does not aver two essential elements of the offence of
133smuggling and therefore, discloses no offence against the accused.
134After having carefully assessed the evidence adduced by the prosecution, I find moreover that there is no
135evidence from the prosecution that the jewellery secured from the accused are ‘dutiable goods’ that is,
136“subject to any duty, excise duty, taxes or related charges;” – see definition at section 2 of the Act – or
137that the accused had the intention to defraud the revenue or to evade a prohibition, restriction or
138regulation. The prosecution has adduced evidence to the effect that the accused was observed entering the
139toilet after Jandoo and coming out of it shortly after Jandoo, with his hand in his pocket and PC
140Mooniaruth securing jewellery from him whilst he was in the parking, but I find that it would be unsafe to
141infer from such evidence that he had such intent as mentioned earlier.
142Learned counsel for the defence has also submitted that the fact that it is the Controller of Assay and not
143the Comptroller of Customs who has determined the value of the jewellery is fatal to the prosecution.
144‘Value’ is defined at section 2 of the Act as follows:
145“(a) in respect of the computation of any duty, means the value as determined in accordance with
146section 18 of this Act;
147(b) …”
148Section 18(1) of the Act specifies that when duty is leviable on goods by reference to their value, the
149value of those goods shall be the transaction value of the goods as determined with section 18A.” and
150thereafter lists the other ways in which the value of the goods can be determined if it cannot be done in
151accordance with section 18A.
152The definition of ‘Transaction value’ at section 2 of the Act is as follows:
153““transaction value”, in relation to imported goods, means the transaction value as determined in
154accordance with section 18A;”
155Section 18A provides that:
156“The transaction value of the goods shall be the price actually paid or payable for the goods when
157sold for export to an importer in Mauritius, adjusted where appropriate in circumstances as may be
158prescribed, and shall in addition include –
159(a) all costs, charges, and expenses incidental to the sale contract and delivery of those
160goods; and
161(b) the loading charges, freight, insurance and other, charges and expenses in respect of
1624
163
164those goods as may be prescribed,
165provided that the prescribed conditions are satisfied.
166It is to be noted that during the trial, upon the defence objecting to the prosecution calling witnesses
167nos.10 and 11 in respect of the value of the alleged smuggled goods, the matter was fixed for Arguments.
168The prosecution then opted to wait for the evidence of witnesses nos.5 and 6 (who was after all not
169called) before offering arguments. However, in the light of the evidence of Mr Goorohoo/W17, the
170prosecution moved for a postponement to take a stand, which stand was that the case was to proceed.
171When the case resumed, witness Goorohoo was not examined any further by the prosecution and was not
172cross-examined either. The prosecution then closed its case.
173Since according to Mr Goorohoo, the Customs Department (now the Director-General, Mauritius
174Revenue Authority/DG, MRA) is the competent authority to assess the value of jewellery smuggled into
175Mauritius and such department did not make the appraisal in the present case, the appraised value given
176to Mr Goorohoo for the calculation of duty paid value is not in accordance with procedure. Furthermore,
177the failure of the prosecution to call witnesses in support of the transaction value of the alleged smuggled
178jewellery is indeed a dearth of evidence in respect of such transaction value.
179It is to be noted that at section 4(2) of the Jewellery Act, it is provided that the Controller of Assay shall:
180“(a) be responsible for -
181(i) the assaying and marking of jewellery; and
182(ii) the verification and grading of precious and semi- precious stones;
183(b) carry out the duties and functions assigned to him under this Act.”
184There is no provision in the Jewellery Act enabling the Controller of Assay to value jewellery secured by
185Customs Officers for the purpose of calculating the duty paid value.
186The defence has further submitted that there was a wrong timing of the purported valuation, in that it
187should have been carried out at the time of the commission of the alleged offence and not thirty days after
188same “as per the report of the Assay Office”.
189The Court is not in presence of the report of the Controller of Assay since the defence objected to its
190production and the prosecution did not insist. Therefore, there is no evidence of the date of the valuation
191of the jewellery.
192Conclusion
193For all the reasons given above, I find that the prosecution has failed to prove the case against the accused
194beyond reasonable doubt.
195I accordingly dismiss the case against the accused.

Catchwords

  1. Statute (Customs Act) — Smuggling — s. 156(1)(a) — Information requirement — Whether information must aver the specific material act of importation and the accused's intent to defraud the revenue or evade a prohibition

Headnote

Facts

The accused, a Customs officer, was observed entering a toilet after a man suspected of smuggling and emerging shortly thereafter with his hand in his pocket. Police stopped the accused in a car park and found him carrying gold jewelry consisting of pendants, bracelets, earrings, rings, and necklaces totalling 669.8g. The jewelry was seized and sent for appraisal. The accused was charged with smuggling under section 156(1)(a) and (2) of the Customs Act, alleging he unlawfully smuggled the goods with intent to defraud the revenue.

Held

  1. An information charging smuggling under section 156(1)(a) of the Customs Act must contain specific averments of the material act of importation, introduction, or exportation and an allegation that the accused acted with intent to defraud the revenue or evade a prohibition, restriction, or regulation; the absence of these essential elements renders the information defective and discloses no offence.

Disposition

Case dismissed. The prosecution has failed to prove the case against the accused beyond reasonable doubt.
