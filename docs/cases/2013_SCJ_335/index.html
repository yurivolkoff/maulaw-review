5IN THE SUPREME COURT OF MAURITIUS
6(COMMERCIAL DIVISION)
7In the matter of:
8Best Luck (Mauritius) Ltd
9Plaintiff
10v/s

13RULING
14At this stage of proceedings I am called upon to rule on a motion to amend the
15defendants’ amended plea, which motion is objected to by the plaintiff.
16In a gist, as can be culled from the plaintiff’s averments in its plaint with summons, it is
17the plaintiff’s case that the first defendant was performing the duties of Administrative Clerk on a
18part time basis for the plaintiff from 2005 to 2008; that whilst he was in employment with Gexim
19Ltd from 1991 to 2005, the first defendant also worked on the accounts of the plaintiff for which
20he was paid an allowance; that following certain irregularities found in the accounts and
21cheques payment of plaintiff in 2008, he stopped working for the plaintiff; that he has “illegally,
22unlawfully, and fraudulently” caused the plaintiff to issue a number of cheques payable to
23himself as well as to the second defendant, a company owned by him. The totals of such
24alleged fraudulent payment to the two defendants respectively are Rs 1,335,000 and Rs
2515,317,500. He is alleged to have abused the plaintiff’s trust by causing it to sign the cheques
26referred to in the plaint and has thereby enriched himself and the second defendant without
27good cause. The claim is therefore for a reimbursement of such alleged fraudulent payment and
28for damages in respect of damage and prejudice allegedly caused to the plaintiff by such
29averred fraudulent acts and doings of the first defendant.
30The defendants have in their plea denied the plaintiff’s averments and explained inter
31alia that from 1999 the plaintiff company had undertaken several Building and Civil Engineering
32
332
34works for the account of Gexim Ltd, which latter was the employer of the first defendant; that
35between 1999 and the end of 2003, he provided several consultancy services to the plaintiff;
36that he did not deal with the accounts of the plaintiff, one Padoomun Boodoo being exclusively
37responsible for such accounts; that besides the first defendant, one Selven Perianen, a Civil
38Engineer, also provided consultancy services, and on several occasion the latter acted with him
39to provide joint consultancy services to the plaintiff; that neither the first defendant nor Selven
40Perianen took any decision on behalf of the plaintiff which always acted by and through one
41Soubass Luckhoo, a Director and the main shareholder of the plaintiff; that the said Soobass
42Luckhoo, although illiterate, was an efficient businessman who took an active part in the
43business of the company; that with the first defendant and Selven Perianen, contributing to the
44plaintiff’s growth, it secured important projects, became profitable and by 2008 on account of its
45performance, the plaintiff acceded to the status of “Grade A Contractor”; that throughout the
46period that defendant No 1 personally or through defendant No 2 provided his services to
47plaintiff, he became closely associated with it, providing it with his personal support, even risking
48his own assets by standing as its guarantor for loans and banking facilities granted to it.
49The defendants have made other averments which need not concern us for the present
50purposes. They have also counterclaimed against the plaintiff Rs 8 million allegedly unpaid and
51due for consultancy services provided by defendant No 2 to the plaintiff at its request.
52The hearing on the merits has started. The first witness, who was called out of turn has
53already deponed. An issue has been canvassed in the course of his examination in chief as to
54the first defendant not being authorised to take employment elsewhere for reward whilst in the
55employ of Gexim Ltd, his employer at material times. The witness has been cross-examined
56and re-examined thereon. At the close of his re-examination, Learned Senior Counsel for the
57defendants has moved to amend paragraph 2 (b) of the plea by adding an averment to the
58effect that the first defendant has been authorised to do certain works for the plaintiff already
59averred in such plea.
60The relevant part of paragraph 2 of the plea reads as follows:
61“2 The defendants deny all the averments contained in paragraphs 2 and 4 of the
62plaint with summons and aver that:-
63
643
65(a) as from the year 1999, plaintiff company undertook several building
66and civil engineering works for the account of Gexmi Ltd;
67(b) between the year 1999 and the end of the year 2003, defendant No 1
68provided several consultancy services to plaintiff;
69The motion for amendment is to add after the last word “plaintiff” at paragraph 2 (b) of
70the plea the following, “and this with the authorisation granted by Gexim”.
71If the amendment is granted, paragraph 2 (b) will therefore read as follows:-
72(c) between year 1999 and the end of the year 2003, defendant No 1 provided
73several consultancy services to plaintiff, and this with the authorisation
74granted by Gexim.
75What will have been added will be the punctuation mark ‘comma’, and the words underlined.
76Learned Senior Counsel for the plaintiff has objected to the amendment mainly on
77account of the stage the proceedings have reached, namely that the case is actually being
78heard. He has also argued that if the motion is allowed the plaintiff may have to ask for
79particulars of the amended plea entailing delay in the proceedings.
80Learned Senior Counsel for the defendants has referred to the well known principles
81obtaining and applied in relation to amendments of pleadings. He has argued that his motion to
82amend results from the fact that the plaintiff has canvassed an issue on which the latter has not
83pleaded, and the defendants have therefore also not pleaded thereon, but now, given the new
84matter in issue the defendants need to plead thereto in order to be able to adduce evidence in
85rebuttal thereof.
86The principles on which amendments of pleadings are allowed or refused are
87well settled. I have sought to list out and summarise them in the case of Hems
88Apparels v. State Bank of Mauritius and Anor [2009 SCJ 419]. The ones which are
89more prominent and relevant to the issues raised here are as follows.
90The basic premise as appears from Rule 17 of our Supreme Court Rules 2000
91is that the Court may “grant an amendment of any pleading, in such manner and on
92such terms as may be just and reasonable, for the purpose of determining the real
93
944
95question in controversy between the parties”. However, the Court will exercise such
96discretion judiciously taking into account inter alia, the nature of the proposed
97amendment, the stage of the proceedings at which, and the purpose for which it is
98made, whether there is bad faith or the intention to overreach on the part of the party
99making it, whether any prejudice is likely to be caused to the other party or parties
100which may not be compensated by an order for costs - (vide Soobhany & Ors v.
101Soobhany & Ors [1989 MR 191], Unmar v. Lagesse [1994 MR 183] and Maxo
102Products v Swan Insurance Co. Ltd [1996 MR 41], where dicta from the English
103cases of G. L. Baker v. Medway Building & Supplies Ltd [1958 1 WLR 1216 at p.
1041231]; [1958 3 ALL E. R. 540, p. 546] and Copper v. Smith [1883 26 Ch. D. 700, at
105pp. 710 – 711] which latter was emphatically approved in Shoe Machinery Co. v.
106Cultham [1896 1 Ch. 108 p. 112]). Those principles have been applied in the more
107recent cases of Reekoye v. Mauritius Union Insurance Co. Ltd & Anor [2004 SCJ
10866], ABC Motors and Ors v. Ngan Hoy Khen Ngan Chee Wang & Ors [2008 SCJ
10925a], and C. Marday & Ors v. B. Marday & Ors. [2008 SCJ 30].
110It is clear from the above authorities that since some decades the modern trend
111has been without doubt, to grant leave to amend even in a case where the motion is
112made at a late hour. However, the Court would be more strict in granting applications
113for amendment at the trial than before the trial in the absence of any explanation as to
114why it could not have been moved for earlier and will generally not grant an
115amendment which will prejudice the right of the opposite party as existing at the time of
116the amendment (Joomun v. Kissoondharry [1977 MR 256] and B Harel & Anor v.
117Société Harel & Cie and Ors [1993 MR 251]. The Court would also not allow an
118amendment which is substantial and raises new issues which are inconsistent with
119those found in the statement of claim (Tive Hive and Ors v. Kam Tim [1953 MR 80]).
120In that context a distinction is to be made between an amendment which purports to
121clarify the issue for determination and one which raises a new or substantial issue for
122the first time (vide Ketteman v. Hansel Properties Ltd [1987 AC 189]), referred to in
123the case of Soobhany v. Soobhany (supra). (Emphasis added).
124On the facts and circumstances of the present matter, Learned Senior Counsel for the
125defendants has argued that paragraph 2 of the defendants’ plea as it now stands is in answer to
126
1275
128the averments of the plaintiff at paragraphs 2 and 4 of the plaint with summons which latter
129paragraphs read as follows:-
130“2. Defendant No 1 was performing the duties of an Administrative Clerk on a part-time
131basis for Plaintiff from the year 2005 to the year 2008.”
132“4. Plaintiff avers that whilst Defendant No 1 was in employment with GEXIM Ltd from
133the year 1991 up to the year 2005, Defendant No 1 also worked on the accounts of Plaintiff and
134for which Defendant No 1 was paid an allowance.”
135It is obvious from such averments of the plaintiff that it is not part of its case that
136defendant No 1 was not authorised by his employer, Gexim, to take up such part time work with
137the plaintiff. And the defendants’ answer as per their plea is in answer to the plaintiff’s
138averments as per its pleadings as they then stood, and now still stand.
139Now, in the course of the examination of the first witness called by the plaintiff, Mr
140Agusti, an employee of Gexim at all material times, the plainfiff sought to establish that the first
141defendant was not authorised to accept work for reward outside his employment with Gexim.
142As rightly submitted by counsel for the defendants, that new element with regard to
143alleged lack of authorisation makes it incumbent upon the defendants to amend their pleadings
144as they propose to do in order to enable them to adduce evidence to rebut the suggestion that
145defendant No 1 acted without authorisation in accepting and doing work for the plaintiff without
146Gexim’s authorisation and in violation of clause 9 of his contract of employment which according
147to the plaintiff stipulates that an employee of Gexim must obtain the express permission of its
148directors before undertaking any private work of any nature whatsoever, for reward.
149Indeed the issue of the need for Gexim’s authorisation was first raised by the plaintiff
150itself whereas the same was not part of its case as per its pleadings. The defendant, it may be
151fairly considered, was taken by surprise, and it might be debarred from adducing evidence in
152rebuttal or qualification of such allegation if it did not seek to adjust its pleadings accordingly.
153For all the above reasons, I hold that the defendants cannot be taken to task for not
154having pleaded on a “fait materiel” not raised initially in the plaintiff’s pleadings. It is legitimate
155for the defence to seek to amend the plea in the manner it has moved to in order to enable it to
156adduce evidence in rebuttal as it seems to propose to do. The objection is consequently
157
1586
159overruled. The motion to amend the plea is granted and it is amended in terms thereof.
160Obviously the defendants cannot be held responsible for any delay resulting from the eventual
161application for particulars of the plea by the plaintiff consequential upon the amendment
162presently granted.
163The matter shall be mentioned before me at the Family Division of the Supreme Court
164on Friday 06 September 2013 at 10.15 hours for it to be set down for continuation.
